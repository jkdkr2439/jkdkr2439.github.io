There is something strange in the toilet. Is it proof?

Proof of what?

Its color may matter if you ask about bleeding. Its shape may matter if you
ask about water and fibre. Its weight may matter to one test and mean almost
nothing to another. The thing does not arrive with a speech pinned to it.

A number, photograph, message, or stain works the same way. It becomes useful
only when joined to a clear claim and a way of telling that claim from its
rivals.

People often bring a fact into a room like a large uncle and expect every
argument to bow. But the same fact can support one sentence, weaken another,
and leave a third untouched.

“Sales rose” may support the claim that more items were sold. It does not by
itself show why, who benefited, or whether people became better off.

Suppose three people get sick after dinner. The empty plate is a fact. The
fact may matter to a question about what they ate. It does not yet prove that
the meal caused the sickness. They may share a drink, a room, or an illness
that began before dinner. To make the plate useful, you must place it beside a
claim and ask what should look different if another claim were true.

Good questions do not bully facts into speaking. They give facts a fair job.
A question is bad when every possible answer is made to support the same
story. It is also bad when the thing being measured has no clear link to what
the question asks.

Before calling something proof, finish this sentence: “It supports this claim
rather than that claim because...” If you cannot finish, keep inspecting. You
do not yet know what work the thing can do.

The toilet contains traces. The question gives those traces a job.
