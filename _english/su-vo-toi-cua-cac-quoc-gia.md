*Kevin T.N a.k.a Lucis The Lord · Original English text*

Gentlemen—and I use the word in its parliamentary sense, where it describes a seat rather more reliably than a character—your invitation has done me an honour of the most modern kind. You have asked a man whom society once considered unfit for a dining room to advise men who consider themselves fit to rearrange the world.

I accepted at once. An artist should never refuse the chance to witness power at close range. From a distance, power resembles destiny. Near at hand, one discovers stationery.

Before me sit the guardians of nations, the custodians of peace, the reluctant authors of necessary wars. Reluctance is among the noblest decorations of public life. It allows a statesman to deplore in the evening what he ordered before luncheon, and to sleep with the tranquillity reserved for men whose verbs have travelled farther than their consciences.

The room itself inspires confidence. The flags stand upright; the silver catches the light; every glass is filled with water no one has had to carry. Outside this hall, a child may spend an afternoon searching rubble for a cup without cracks. The contrast should not trouble us. Civilization has always understood proportion. It gives crystal to those who discuss suffering and broken vessels to those required to practise it.

You have assembled to speak of war and children. War usually receives the longer speech. Children have the advantage of being shorter.

Let us begin, then, with a child.

She is seven years old. I shall not give her a nationality, since each delegation would immediately decide whether her death was tragic, regrettable, exaggerated, strategically relevant, or committed by the other side. Her name is of no assistance to policy. She has drawn a house with four windows, although the house in which she lives has only two. Children are the last architects who improve the world without first demolishing somebody else's home.

On the morning chosen for her by history, she sits beneath a table while aircraft pass overhead. Her mother tells her the noise is moving away. Mothers are expected to lie beautifully in wartime; it is one of the few forms of propaganda performed without a salary. The girl holds a blue pencil. When the ceiling falls, the pencil enters the dust before her hand does.

Hours later, a soldier finds it. He puts it in his pocket because its colour has survived. The child is entered elsewhere.

Here, perhaps, she becomes a civilian casualty. In another office she is collateral damage. At a press conference she is an allegation awaiting verification. By dinner she has achieved the highest distinction available to the powerless: she has become a number important enough to be denied.

Language has behaved impeccably. No blood has reached the minutes.

I see that some of you smiled earlier. I am grateful. Laughter is the sincerest form of diplomacy; it reveals an agreement before the lawyers have weakened it. Yet I would ask you to remember what amused you. The joke was carried here by the same machinery that carried the order outward. Only the direction differed.

We are told that war is sometimes necessary. Necessity has had an extraordinary career. It began as a condition and rose to become a sovereign. It signs decrees, closes borders, enters bedrooms, and has never once been required to stand for election. When a minister says, “We had no choice,” he pays necessity the compliment of surrendering his office while retaining its privileges.

Choice, meanwhile, is sent to the front.

There a young soldier receives an instruction polished by many hands. Advance. Secure. Neutralise. The words are admirable: brief, disciplined, incapable of screaming. He sees a window, a movement, perhaps the blue sleeve of a child. Behind him stand his comrades, his training, the law, the fear of shame, and a nation that has lent him its courage on the condition that he use it in the approved direction.

If he fires, you will praise obedience. If he refuses, you may praise conscience fifty years later, when conscience has become harmless enough for a monument. Governments are generous toward dead dissent. The living variety causes delays.

I do not say that the soldier possesses no choice. Such mercy would erase him as thoroughly as military language erases the child. His choice exists inside a machine designed to make one direction easy, another punishable, and both available for later speeches. Responsibility survives coercion; it merely changes weight. Your reports prefer equal columns. The soul has always been badly formatted.

The order itself enjoys a more comfortable journey. It begins as policy, dresses as strategy, travels as coordinates, and arrives as heat. At every border it surrenders a little meaning. The minister does not choose the house; the analyst does not release the weapon; the pilot does not know the child; the manufacturer has sold only an object; the citizen has paid only a tax. Responsibility is distributed with democratic elegance. Everyone receives a fragment. The child receives the whole.

This is considered progress. The ancient tyrant had to look upon the man he condemned. Modern government has rescued authority from that unpleasant intimacy.

You are all fathers, or uncles, or at least the owners of childhood portraits. I have no wish to doubt your tenderness. Doubt would be unnecessary. A man may kiss his daughter before sleep and approve the bombing of another man's daughter before breakfast. The heart is a spacious house, though patriotism has become remarkably skilled at renting it by the room.

We call this partial tenderness natural. Nature has often served as society's most obedient barrister. Whenever we tire of defending a custom, we instruct nature to plead it. Yet civilization, whose praises occupy so much of your stationery, ought to begin precisely where affection ceases to be private property.

If a child's value depends upon the flag above her roof, humanity is merely nationalism in evening dress.

There are, I know, practical objections. Practical men are invaluable in a crisis; they explain why every preventable horror was unavoidable. They will tell us that enemies hide among civilians, that hesitation costs lives, that intelligence is imperfect, that deterrence saves more children than it kills. Some claims may even be true. Truth is quite capable of entering bad company.

So ask the questions that truth requires. Who selected the target? What evidence survived challenge? Which alternatives were rejected? Who bears the risk of uncertainty? What number of foreign children purchases the possible safety of one child at home? At what exchange rate does a minister cease to call the arithmetic tragic and begin to call it policy?

Do not answer with purity. Politics has none, and children cannot eat it. Answer with mechanisms, names, signatures, limits, and the courage to leave a decision unsigned.

You frequently speak of protecting the future. The future must find this exhausting. It is invoked at every podium and buried after every campaign. Children appear in your speeches as heirs to peace and in your calculations as variables of war. A society that loves children chiefly in the future has discovered the ideal child: one who cannot interrupt the present.

Perhaps this explains our passion for memorials. A living child asks for bread, medicine, safety, and an inconvenient revision of policy. A bronze child asks only for a pedestal. States are often moved by suffering once it has acquired suitable architecture.

And artists—since I must not grant my own profession diplomatic immunity—are capable of another indecency. We take the broken house, the bloodied shoe, the mother's unfinished sentence, and arrange them until anguish achieves style. Sometimes form restores the relation that official language has severed. Sometimes beauty gives pain a second audience. Sometimes we merely teach horror how to enter a drawing room.

The distinction is severe. A beautiful sentence about a dead child may preserve her from the number; it may also replace her with the writer. Art has clean hands only in galleries. In life, it must account for what it touches.

You may wonder what an artist can ask of statesmen. I shall ask very little. Large requests encourage ceremonial answers.

When an operation is proposed, require every euphemism to name its body. Let *neutralise* state who will cease breathing. Let *acceptable loss* name the person expected to accept it. Let *collateral* produce a family photograph. Place the predicted dead at the table before the living vote. Empty chairs are vulgar in a banquet hall. That is why they are useful.

Require the author of an order to remain its reader after the result arrives. Prohibit the promotion of responsibility into abstraction. Allow no report to use the passive voice until the dead have been restored to the active one.

Above all, distrust the cleanliness of the room. Blood on a soldier's sleeve may reveal a crime. Its absence from a minister's cuff proves only the excellence of the service.

Soon I shall finish and you will applaud. It is the ancient settlement between art and power: the artist is permitted to tell the truth, and power is permitted to treat the truth as entertainment. Then you will rise. You will praise candour. You will shake one another's hands beneath these admirable flags.

The gesture is civilized, reassuring, almost beautiful.

Before you perform it, remember the blue pencil in the dust. Remember that somewhere a mother is still lying to a child because the truth has your signature upon it. Remember that clean hands are easiest to admire when someone else's child has carried away the stain.

Gentlemen, I do not ask you to leave this hall innocent. You arrived that way.

I ask only that, when you wash your hands tonight, you recognise the ceremony.
