For several days now, people have been passing around the story of an A.I. company that bought books, cut off their spines, scanned every page, and discarded the paper remains. That was enough to summon George Orwell, open the gates of every dystopia, and place human civilization on a stretcher. A hydraulic blade passed through several million book spines and, in the public imagination, immediately became a pyre for knowledge.

Images have a power of their own. People may remain unmoved by a thirty-page judgment yet tremble before a torn cover. Paper has a smell. A spine makes a sound when it breaks. A deleted file produces no noise sufficiently tragic, whereas a heap of pulped books looks very much like a crime, especially once we have supplied the music.

But revulsion is not an argument.

## They Bought Them

The first question should have been brief: who owned those books?

Anthropic bought them. It did not borrow them, accept them on consignment, or receive a commission to preserve them on behalf of humanity. The company paid for each physical copy. Once the transaction was complete, the seller had the money and the buyer had the book. As owner of the object purchased, the buyer could read it, store it, resell it, give it away, cover its margins in notes, tear it up for a stage prop, put it beneath a table leg, cut off the spine for scanning, or place it in a recycling bin.

Buying a book does not amount to signing a perpetual adoption agreement with civilization.

Most of the outraged understand this principle in every other setting. They buy a shirt and cut off its sleeves. They buy a table and shorten its legs. They buy a newspaper and wrap fish in it. No one arrives in tears to announce that the history of journalism has been desecrated. The moment an object takes the shape of a book, however, ownership disappears beneath a sacred mist. The question is no longer who owns the object, but who has dared touch the relic.

The confusion advances through four convenient steps:

*I treasure books. I could not bear to destroy my books. Therefore, no one may destroy theirs. If they do, they are enemies of knowledge.*

The final three steps do not follow from the first. They are personal preference dressed as universal obligation.

Anyone wishing to make a moral accusation must identify the duty that has been violated. Were these books entrusted property? Was Anthropic bound by contract to return or preserve them? Were they unique manuscripts, protected heritage, historical evidence, or objects sold under restrictions on disposal? Did the company use its power to prevent others from owning the same titles?

If none of this is true, “immoral” has been reduced to a very poor meaning: they did something I dislike to an object that was not mine.

“It pains me to see it” is not a branch of law. It is not yet a moral philosophy either.

## Three Acts Forced into One

The legal story contains three different acts, but social media prefers to compress them into a single photograph.

First, Anthropic bought millions of physical books, many of them used, cut off the spines, scanned them in full, and retained the digital versions in an internal library. The paper copies were discarded after scanning.

Second, some copies were used to train language models. In this particular record, the court found the training to be fair use because it was highly transformative and there was no allegation that the models had released exact copies or infringing imitations to the public.

Third, before the physical-book campaign, Anthropic had downloaded more than seven million books from pirate repositories and kept them in a central library, including books never subsequently used for training. The court did not allow the purpose of training to wash clean the means by which that library had been acquired. Buying a physical copy after taking a pirated one does not legalize the pirated copy. The case was later settled, with an obligation to destroy material downloaded from LibGen, PiLiMi, and related copies, except where retention was legally required.

The court did not order Anthropic to destroy physical books as a condition of digitization. The company chose destructive scanning. The ruling was narrower: in these circumstances, Anthropic bought one paper copy and replaced it with one internal digital copy. The conversion did not increase the number of copies in the library, was not intended for external sale or distribution, and saved space while making search easier. The source copy was destroyed and the replacement remained. The court treated that conversion as fair use.

This does not mean destroying an original purifies every act of copying. Ownership of a physical copy and copyright in the work are different layers. You may burn the novel you bought; that does not entitle you to print a million new copies for sale. You may cut the spine from your book; the creation and use of its digital version must still be assessed under copyright law.

But precisely because those layers differ, a buyer's disposal of paper cannot be turned into a moral crime merely by the emotional sound of the word “book.”

## A Cutter Is Not a Pyre

Historical book burning was not frightening because fire touched paper. It was frightening because power wished to determine which words would be allowed to remain.

A state that hunts texts, prohibits copying, kills or imprisons readers, destroys schools, erases languages, and severs routes of transmission is doing something entirely different from a purchaser replacing a paper copy with a digital one. In the first case, destroying the carrier is one link in removing an idea from society. In the second, the sequence of signs remains intact and may even become easier to search; only its material carrier has changed from paper to memory.

The photographs may look alike. The mechanisms do not.

Someone smashing a statue to prevent others from worshipping it is not the same as its owner crushing it to clear a warehouse. A government burning records to erase guilt is not the same as an office scanning records before destroying paper on schedule. If we look only at mechanical action and ignore actor, purpose, surviving copies, access, and consequence, every office shredder becomes a miniature Qin Shi Huang.

The comparison does not make us more moral. It merely shows how easily an image leads us by the hand.

## How Many Things Is a Book?

The worship of books relies on a simple trick: it collapses every layer into one.

A book is first a carrier: paper, ink, glue, thread, and cover. Upon that carrier is a text, a sequence of signs that can be reproduced on another material. The text carries information: propositions, images, instructions, stories. But information is not automatically knowledge. Knowledge requires a subject capable of understanding, testing, relating, and using it. Beyond that lies cultural memory: variants, provenance, annotations, readers' marks, circulation history, and the community that gave the text meaning.

These layers are related. They are not identical.

If one ordinary edition is pulped while thousands of copies and an accurate digital version remain, that carrier has been lost; the text has not. If a unique manuscript is scanned at high resolution but its marginalia, paper, ink, binding, and history of ownership are discarded, the words may remain while part of its material memory is lost. If a library keeps a million books intact but has no catalogue, no one knows the language, and no one is permitted to read them, the objects remain while knowledge may already be dead.

Knowledge is not contained in a book as wine is contained in a bottle. Break the bottle and the wine may spill. Destroy one copy of a book and what was written need not disappear, provided the network of transmission survives.

The reverse is equally true: keeping books on shelves does not mean knowledge has been kept. Many households fill walls with books mainly to preserve a background. Many institutions hold uncatalogued, undigitized collections that no one has the authority or ability to read. Such rooms contain a great deal of paper and very little intellectual life.

The book is a technology for transmitting knowledge. It is not knowledge itself.

## The Bamboo Slips Burned; Texts Found Other Roads

Consider the example invoked whenever someone wishes to elevate a bin of scrap paper into an apocalypse: the burning of books under the Qin.

The horror was not that bamboo slips were flammable. Power sought to impoverish comparison. Records, doctrines, and memories that might be used to criticize the new order were targeted; control of texts accompanied control of those who transmitted them. The purpose was not to save warehouse space. It was to leave one version of the world without a rival.

Even here, however, a text was not identical to each bundle of slips. Traditions survived in hidden copies, memorization, copies beyond the reach of pursuit, and objects buried in tombs. More than two thousand years later, discoveries of Warring States bamboo manuscripts returned works absent from the received tradition, alternative versions, and previously unknown mathematical, musical, and historical texts.

Archaeology demonstrates two things at once.

First, destroying carriers can cause real loss. Some texts survive only in fragments; their reading contexts, interpretations, and surrounding arguments cannot be excavated intact.

Second, knowledge survives through dispersion. A bundle of bamboo is not immortal. Networks of copying, concealment, memory, and retransmission give a text more than one route of escape.

The lesson for digitization is not “never destroy anything bearing words.” It is this: never place the entire power to preserve, decode, and grant access in one hand.

## Alexandria Did Not Die in One Night

The Library of Alexandria is even more useful than Qin Shi Huang to the popular imagination. People prefer a great fire because fire provides a culprit, a climax, and an anniversary. An institution declining through lost funding, dwindling scribal labour, prolonged war, decaying materials, and centuries of indifference is harder to turn into cinema.

But Alexandria was not a hard drive containing all ancient knowledge and erased in a single night. Its collections suffered several disasters; the institution itself gradually weakened. Papyrus required no invading army in order to die. Humidity, insects, and time worked with patience. Unless someone copied an old text onto a new carrier, disappearance was merely a date waiting to arrive.

A classical work reached us not because its first scroll was preserved, but because generation after generation copied, edited, quoted, translated, taught, and carried it to another centre. What preserves a text is a chain of labour, not the magic of paper.

If Alexandria deserves remembrance, it is not as the emblem of one night in which fire swallowed knowledge whole. It reminds us that libraries die when societies cease to maintain the work that keeps collections meaningful.

Sometimes the building burns only after the library has long been dead.

## When Fire Truly Enters Memory

To leap from this to “destroying books never matters” would merely exchange one stupidity for its symmetrical twin.

There are moments when destroying the carrier truly destroys knowledge. Not because every sheet of paper is sacred, but because some objects are irreplaceable links.

When Maya manuscripts were destroyed during religious coercion, the loss did not stop at paper. The surviving pre-Columbian codices can be counted on one hand. The destroyed books belonged to a world whose readers, rituals, institutions, and systems of interpretation were simultaneously being suppressed. Fire struck the unique copy, the reading community, and the possibility of transmission at once. No scan existed as replacement. No other shop sold the same edition.

When the National and University Library of Bosnia and Herzegovina in Sarajevo was deliberately destroyed in 1992, the targets were not merely bundles of paper. Records, memories, and traces of a community were under attack. Destroying the library formed part of making another people's past harder to prove, remember, and pass on.

That is when destruction of an object becomes epistemic violence.

The distinction is not mysterious. Ask: is this the only copy? Does an accurate copy exist elsewhere? Do people still know how to read it? Does the interpretive code survive? Does the community retain access? Is the object being destroyed to clear space, to change format, or to make a voice disappear?

Fire does not destroy knowledge by itself. Fire destroys knowledge when it cuts every road by which knowledge might return.

## Rare Does Not Mean Valuable

Part of the panic rests on the idea that every book, simply by having become a book, contains an irreplaceable portion of human excellence.

No. There are many bad books.

That is not an insult to books. It is the ordinary statistical consequence of every form of production. There are bad scientific papers, bad films, bad songs, bad code, bad dissertations, and naturally bad books. A text does not acquire epistemic quality by becoming long enough to bind.

Nor should sales become the measure of value. A poorly selling book may be bad; it may also be highly specialized, mistimed, written in a language few people read, or still waiting for the right reader. A market tells us how many people paid. It does not tell us how true a proposition is.

The point is simpler: “rare” and “valuable” are different judgments.

Something may be rare because no one wishes to keep it. A book may have few copies because the publisher correctly guessed that almost no one needed it. Conversely, a work printed in millions may contain great literature, while a unique note by an unknown person may become priceless as the final evidence of an event.

Scarcity matters when joined to irreplaceability. An ordinary copy in a shipment of used books does not become heritage merely because an A.I. company's scanner is about to touch it.

A lump of excrement rarely appears in the middle of a living room. When it does, few people establish a conservation fund.

## There Is No Need to Canonize Anthropic

Some will assume that rejecting the language of “book burning” requires defending Anthropic as a benefactor of knowledge. It does not.

Ownership is not a certificate of total moral innocence. An act may be legal while producing consequences worth discussing. A company may legally buy millions of used books, digitize them, and keep the data privately. The political question then lies not in the paper corpses but in the redistribution of access.

Books once dispersed through shops, clearance warehouses, and second-hand markets are drawn into a private library. The paper leaves circulation; the digital version is not opened to the public. The content may not be lost, but access has contracted. The ability to inspect scan quality, OCR, provenance, variants, and integrity depends on an organization with no duty to exist forever. If the company fails, changes strategy, locks the data, or deletes the archive, society has little power to intervene.

That is a real problem.

We may also ask whether bulk purchasing depletes particular second-hand supplies; whether the process identifies copies with material value, signatures, annotations, or provenance; whether the digital collection has mechanisms for long-term preservation; and how the power of a model grows when it absorbs texts outsiders can no longer inspect.

These are questions of memory infrastructure, data power, and private ownership. They are harder than posting a photograph of shredded paper beneath a sentence from Orwell. Because they are harder, they are less popular.

People prefer condemning a cutter to analysing a regime of access.

## Why Not Resell or Open the Collection?

Some ask why Anthropic did not rebind the books and sell them, or give them to libraries. The first answer is that it was not obliged to.

A business does not automatically acquire a duty to build an expensive, risky process merely to satisfy the feelings of people outside the transaction. The books had been debound for scanning. Sorting, rebinding, storing, shipping, grading, and selling each copy might cost more than could be recovered. Anyone who believes the work worthwhile may organize it, fund it, and propose cooperation. “Why do they not spend their money doing what I find beautiful?” is an easy question to ask with someone else's money.

Likewise, opening the entire digital collection to the public sounds noble until we remember that copyright persists, data costs money to create, and a business has no natural reason to donate an advantage to its competitors. One may argue that law should establish deposit, preservation, or research-access mechanisms for very large data collections. But that is a policy proposal. It requires rules, scope, cost, and responsibility. It cannot be replaced by the slogan that knowledge must be free.

Authors and publishers are people too. They cannot live on memorial speeches for civilization.

## A Civilization Far Too Easy to Collapse

The most comic feature of the panic is the civilization it imagines: magnificent, yet strangely fragile. It has survived war, censorship, insects, floods, changes of script, shifts of language, and thousands of years of erroneous copying. Yet apparently a company need only cut the spines from books it has bought and the whole intellectual inheritance of humankind begins to sway.

If our knowledge depends on preserving every used printed copy forever, that civilization was dead before the blade began moving.

A civilization lives not because no carrier is ever destroyed. It lives because important content has many copies; because languages still have readers; because texts are debated and tested; because libraries have catalogues; because archives have budgets; because authors are paid; because students have access; and because when an old material decays, someone transfers its content to a new one.

Paper is not immortal. Neither are hard drives. Formats become obsolete, encryption keys disappear, servers stop, companies fail. Preservation has never meant keeping every object unchanged. Preservation is the organization of continuity through the deaths that every carrier must undergo.

So criticize Anthropic if it took pirated books. Criticize the concentration of intellectual power in closed data stores. Ask who may read, who may verify, who benefits, and who is paid. Propose archival duties, research access, or procedures for identifying rare copies where there is sufficient reason.

But before organizing a funeral for paper copies someone else bought, state clearly where their duty to preserve them came from.

If that cannot be answered, do not call the cutter a pyre. Do not call warehouse disposal censorship. And do not place a carrier of information upon your head and mistake yourself for a defender of knowledge.

Civilization does not reside in a book's spine.

It resides in the ability to know what must be carried forward - and why.
