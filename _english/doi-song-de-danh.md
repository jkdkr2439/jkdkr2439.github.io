There is a sentence many of us pronounce more often than our own names.

After this is over.

After the examination, the promotion, the debt, the illness, the difficult year. After the house has been bought, the body repaired, the parents reassured, the children grown, the mind disciplined, the bank account taught not to tremble. Then, we say, life may begin.

We say it quietly because it sounds responsible. Folly announces itself with trumpets; postponement arrives carrying a calendar.

I am speaking to those of you who have become excellent custodians of a future self. You feed this person with your mornings. You insure him with your evenings. You defend her from embarrassment, uncertainty and want. You have furnished a whole existence for this fortunate stranger, though you have never met.

You may object that preparation is necessary. It is. Winter does not become a philosophy merely because one has failed to buy a coat. Rent remains stubbornly literal. A sick parent cannot be nursed with an aphorism, and no elegance of spirit has yet persuaded a creditor to admire an empty account. There are duties which are not illusions. There are prisons whose bars are made of invoices, bodies and other people's need.

But necessity has a peculiar appetite. Give it a room and it requests the house. Give it Monday and it develops plans for the decade. What begins as endurance becomes etiquette; what was once an emergency acquires office hours. Eventually we no longer postpone life because circumstances demand it. We postpone because postponement has become the only form of life in which we know how to behave.

Consider the good shirt kept for an occasion important enough to deserve it. It waits in darkness while its owner attends birthdays in something less good. Years pass. The body changes its government. The shirt survives moths, fashions and several versions of hope, only to be discovered one afternoon with a collar too narrow for the neck it was meant to honour.

No tragedy has occurred. That is why the scene is tragic.

The shirt was not destroyed. It was preserved perfectly from use.

We do something similar with tenderness. We save a visit until we have more time, an apology until our pride can afford it, a confession until the result is safe. We postpone learning an instrument until we can practise properly, walking through our own city until we have travelled elsewhere, reading the book until the mind is rested enough to deserve it. We even defer grief, as though sorrow were an appointment at which punctuality would be indecent.

The future becomes a magnificent warehouse. Into it we place everything too alive for the present.

And because the warehouse is imaginary, nothing inside appears to decay.

This is the fraud. The life kept in reserve is not standing still. It is being spent on the act of reservation.

Time does not distinguish between the afternoon used to live and the afternoon used to prepare for living. It accepts both without comment. The clock is admirably free of moral preference. It will consume an hour of fear with the same appetite it brings to an hour of joy.

You may have noticed that postponed life creates an unusual kind of fatigue. Ordinary labour exhausts the body; suspended existence exhausts the meaning of labour. One can bear a hard road when it leads somewhere. What becomes unbearable is the road that keeps extending its destination in order to justify itself.

First you work so that one day you may be secure. Then security requires a larger sum. The larger sum requires a position. The position requires availability. Availability consumes the hours security was meant to protect. At last you own every instrument of peace except the habit of being peaceful.

This is not ambition's fault. Ambition is merely a flame; it can cook a meal or burn the kitchen. The trouble begins when achievement ceases to be something one does and becomes the customs office through which every pleasure must pass. Rest must declare its usefulness. Friendship must present a return. A walk requires a productivity alibi. Even sleep is praised not for its darkness, but for the employee it will manufacture by morning.

Under such a government, the present is never innocent. It must justify its existence to the future.

Perhaps some of you are now expecting the familiar command: seize the day. It is a charming instruction, especially when issued by people whose rent has already been seized by someone else. I will not tell you to abandon prudence, resign theatrically, spend tomorrow's food on tonight's wine, or treat dependence as cowardice. Recklessness is not presence. A person fleeing every obligation is no more alive than one buried beneath them.

The question is smaller and less flattering.

Which parts of your postponement are still payments to reality, and which have become tribute to fear?

Reality can usually name its price. Fear prefers a subscription.

Reality says: I need three more months. Fear says: not yet.

Reality asks for a sum, a date, a skill, a conversation. Fear invents conditions whose completion produces new conditions. It is an ingenious administrator. It never denies your application for a life. It merely requests one further document.

Ask yourself what evidence would be sufficient. How much money would count as enough? What exact event would grant permission? Which person's approval would release you? If the answer has no number, date, act or boundary, you may not have a plan. You may have built a religion around the word later.

Every religion needs a sacrifice. This one sacrifices the person who is here to the person who may arrive.

The future self is always astonishingly competent. He rises early without resentment. She speaks the necessary truth with perfect composure. This creature has money, courage, health and a clean desk. Naturally we entrust everything to such a person. The present self, by contrast, is tired, compromised and badly dressed.

There is only one defect in this arrangement: the future self never appears as promised. When tomorrow arrives, it enters wearing today's face.

We are therefore not choosing between an imperfect life now and a perfect life later. We are choosing what kinds of imperfection we are willing to inhabit. The unfinished person who begins will be embarrassed. The unfinished person who waits will be safe from embarrassment and gradually become intimate with regret.

Regret is rarely dramatic at first. It does not break down the door. It notices small domestic facts. The number no longer in the telephone. The street visited after the shop has closed. The song one can recognise but never learned to play. The parent whose stories were postponed until memory misplaced their endings. A chair in a restaurant across from no one, reserved for a conversation that both people once assumed could happen any time.

We imagine that lost possibilities disappear. They do not. They often remain nearby after losing their doors.

That is why the life in reserve can feel so crowded. It fills with rooms one may still see but can no longer enter.

What, then, should be done?

Not everything. The demand to live fully is itself one of modern life's more exhausting forms of tyranny. No human being can answer every call, develop every gift, love every person, visit every country or convert every hour into significance. A life is not enlarged by refusing to exclude. It becomes a life by choosing what it is willing to lose.

The task is not to abolish postponement. It is to prevent postponement from becoming anonymous.

Give waiting a name. Give it a cost. Give it a date on which it must defend itself again.

If you must work for five years, know what the five years are buying. If you delay a conversation, admit what the delay may destroy. If a pleasure must wait, do not insult it by pretending it is immortal. If duty takes the evening, let it take the evening honestly; do not allow it to claim that the evening never belonged to you.

Above all, reserve some small thing from reservation.

Wear the shirt before the occasion becomes worthy. Take the walk while the city is still the city in which you live. Ask the question before you have composed the personality of someone brave enough to ask it. Give an hour to the work that may fail. Sit beside the person whose need cannot be made efficient.

These acts will not rescue you from time. Nothing does. Their value is precisely that they do not rescue you. They return you to the place where time is actually being lost.

One day, many of the things now obstructing you will indeed be over. The debt will close. The office will replace you. The body will settle one argument by beginning another. The difficult year will become a sentence told at dinner. You will finally reach the clearing toward which you have carried so much of yourself.

Be careful.

You may discover that waiting has followed you there.

For life does not begin when circumstances grant permission. Circumstances have no such authority. Life has already begun, with indecent haste, under imperfect conditions, without consulting the version of you assigned to supervise it.

The future is not where your life is kept.

It is one of the places where your present goes to disappear.
