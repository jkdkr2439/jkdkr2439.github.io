I once believed that whatever was right could be done. I believed it for so long, and with such certainty, that it never occurred to me that one day I might have to sit down and ask myself: what if I cannot do it?

The question did not come from outside. No one asked it of me. It rose from within, like groundwater seeping through a hairline crack in a concrete floor I had mistaken for solid. At first, there was only a trace of damp. Then a spreading stain. Then a small pool, until one day I stepped into it and felt the cold soak through.

There were things I believed to be right. I believed in them as though they were the pillars of a house. I believed one should tell the truth. That one should stand up. That one should live honestly with oneself. Those convictions were beautiful, spotless, and easy to display like an ideal map. But a map is not a road.

The first time I failed to do what I believed was right, I thought myself a coward. The second time, I thought myself lazy. By the third, I had begun to suspect that I was no longer the person I had imagined myself to be. A voice whispered: *You are pretending. You do not truly believe. If you did, you would have acted.*

Then one day I understood something very simple: a lamp cannot light the whole room when its battery is failing. And I, who had spent years trying to cast light, had never stopped to see how much power I had left.

The body does not speak in words. It speaks in exhaustion, in sleepless nights, in the weight that settles over each waking morning. And instead of listening, I kept unfolding the map, studying it, and blaming myself for being unable to move.

Some things are right, but not yet right in time. Some things are right, but not for the person I am at this moment. I learned to tell the difference - not through reason, but by lying still and feeling it.

I saw myself beneath a sky crowded with stars, each star a right thing I had not managed to do. They did not accuse me. They merely shone, and waited.
