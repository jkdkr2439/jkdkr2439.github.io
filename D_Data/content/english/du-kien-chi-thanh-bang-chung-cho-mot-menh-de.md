A red light flashes on a laboratory instrument. To the technician it may
indicate contamination. To the engineer it may indicate a failed sensor. To a
visitor it indicates only that the machine has produced a red light.

The datum has not changed. Its evidential role has.

People often speak of evidence as though it were a substance lying inside
facts. One side has evidence; the other lacks it. But a datum becomes evidence
only within a relation among a claim, its alternatives, a method, and
background assumptions.

> Evidence is not merely what has been observed. It is what an observation
> does to the comparative standing of claims.

## The relational form

Suppose `D` is a datum and `H` a hypothesis. `D` supports `H` when `D` is more
expected if `H` is true than it would be under relevant alternatives. The
exact interpretation of support may vary, but three consequences follow.

First, no datum identifies its own target claim. Second, alternatives matter.
Third, background conditions determine whether the path from datum to claim is
reliable.

Wet streets may support the claim that it rained, unless street-cleaning is a
live alternative. A positive test may support infection, depending on how the
test behaves among infected and uninfected cases. A document may support an
historical claim, depending on provenance, authorship, purpose, and alteration.

## Evidence and explanation are not identical

A fact may count against one claim without explaining what actually happened.
Finding that a key does not fit weakens the claim that it opened the door. It
does not identify who entered.

This matters because arguments often rush from refutation to possession. Once
an opponent's account fails, the surviving favorite is declared proved. But
the space of alternatives may be larger than the debate.

## Evidence can be dependent

Ten reports may appear stronger than one. If all ten copy the same source,
their agreement adds little. Three instruments may appear independent while
sharing one calibration procedure. Several studies may draw from overlapping
data.

Quantity counts only after dependence is reconstructed. Repetition can
increase visibility without increasing evidential diversity.

## Absence needs a detection model

Absence of evidence becomes evidence of absence only when the observation
process was likely to detect the thing if it existed. Failing to hear an
elephant in a quiet room matters. Failing to hear a mouse in a storm may not.

This simple principle defeats two opposite errors. One side treats every
silence as disproof. The other declares that no absence could ever matter.
Both avoid the real question: what probability of detection did the search
create?

## Evidence can be strong and still misleading

Evidence changes rational confidence; it does not guarantee truth. An
unlikely coincidence can occur. A reliable instrument can fail. A strong
sample can be unrepresentative by chance.

This is not a defect in evidence. It is the difference between support and
entailment. Inquiry must remain capable of saying both “the evidence strongly
favored this conclusion” and “the conclusion was false.”

## Evidence has custody

A datum rarely arrives naked. Someone selected the instrument, decided when to
measure, cleaned the record, named the variables, and preserved one output
rather than another. None of this automatically corrupts the evidence. It
creates a chain of custody.

The phrase is borrowed from law because it forces an unfashionable question:
who had the thing, and what could have happened while it passed through their
hands? A spreadsheet may look cleaner than a bloodstained shirt, but its
history can be equally consequential. Columns disappear. Cases are excluded.
Outliers become errors after the result is known. A graph begins at a convenient
number and gives a small change the posture of a cliff.

Custody should not be confused with conspiracy. Most transformations are
ordinary and necessary. Precisely for that reason they must be recorded. Error
often enters wearing work clothes.

## The theatrical datum

Some observations dominate discussion because they are vivid enough to perform
their own interpretation. A dramatic photograph, a leaked sentence, or a
single ruined case may deserve attention. It does not deserve exemption from
comparison.

The proper response to vivid evidence is not emotional sterilization. Ask what
claim the case supports, how it was selected, what rate or background it sits
inside, and whether a less visible comparison changes its force. A datum can be
morally urgent and statistically unrepresentative at the same time. Human
thought becomes dangerous when it insists that only one of those sentences may
be true.

## An evidence audit

Ask:

1. What exact claim is being supported?
2. Against which alternatives?
3. Through what method was the datum produced?
4. Which background assumptions connect datum and claim?
5. How independent are repeated observations?
6. Was the process capable of detecting what it failed to find?
7. Does the evidence support, explain, or entail?

Data do not walk into a conclusion. They must be carried by inference. The
next chapter examines the different bridges by which thought crosses beyond
what it directly observes.
