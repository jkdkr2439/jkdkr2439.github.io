<p>One morning a small branch found itself inside the dark mouth of a rifle. No one knew whether it had volunteered. The petals trembled; the metal did not. From a distance they looked reconciled, which is the advantage distance has always enjoyed over truth. People gathered around the scene and felt, with considerable relief, that tenderness had finally found the correct address.</p>

<p>The air carried two scents. One came from crushed leaves. The other was older: iron, smoke, and the faint sourness of hatred kept too long in a closed room. The flower did not defeat that smell. It merely gave the nose somewhere more polite to rest. Human beings call this hope when they are generous and public relations when they have seen the invoice.</p>

<p>At dinner we perform a similar kindness. A cook lays a green sprig upon meat and the plate suddenly appears to have enjoyed a childhood in the countryside. Nothing beneath the garnish has returned to life, but appetite is easily impressed by good manners. The branch in the rifle offered the same courtesy. It did not deny what the weapon was; it made looking at it less embarrassing.</p>

<p>Perfume has an equally distinguished career. It cannot close a wound, yet it can rescue the visitor from the vulgarity of smelling blood. Much public virtue is composed in this way. We place something fragrant near what hurts, then congratulate the fragrance for behaving like medicine. The wounded are expected to appreciate the improvement in atmosphere.</p>

<p>Power has never objected to ornament. A blade may be blessed, a prison gate decorated, a command delivered in a cultivated voice. Ceremony does not weaken force; it teaches force where to place its elbows at dinner. A flower is therefore safe until it begins asking who loaded the rifle. Beauty may attend the palace. Curiosity is searched at the door.</p>

<p>Still, relief is not false merely because it is incomplete. A pain that pauses gives the body one quiet minute in which to choose its next movement. The branch may soften a finger, embarrass an order, or make one witness unable to forget what nearly happened. A small interruption sometimes enters history by a door through which grand intentions could not fit. The mistake is not loving the moment. The mistake is asking the moment to perform all the years that follow it.</p>

<p>Everyone in the picture receives a modest reward. Authority discovers that it can appear gentle without surrendering anything. The idealist enjoys victory before consequences arrive. The writer obtains a symbol obedient enough to fit inside a sentence. The reader acquires sorrow, anger, and redemption without leaving the chair. Even hatred gains something: hidden beneath petals, it may continue breathing without having to defend its manners.</p>

<p>Only later does the rude question appear. When the branch is gone, what has learned to behave differently? If the answer is merely the eye, then the image changed a witness. That is already something. If a hand, an order, or a future refusal has also changed, the image has travelled farther than beauty usually travels. If nothing has changed, the rifle has lost only a morning, and history has gained another lovely excuse.</p>

<p>Perhaps peace is not the flower and not the rifle. Perhaps it is the hand that discovers it no longer wishes to hold either one for an audience. Until then, the branch will continue blooming bravely in photographs, and the weapon will continue displaying the patience of anything that knows flowers do not live long.</p>

<p><strong>Hatred rarely fears beauty. It fears beauty that remembers where the bullets are kept.</strong></p>
