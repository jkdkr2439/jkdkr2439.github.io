A celebrated surgeon speaks about hospital infection. The title “surgeon”
matters. If the same person speaks about monetary policy, the title remains
impressive but its evidential force changes.

Prestige travels more easily than competence.

> Expertise is not a permanent property attached to a person. It is a bounded
> relation among a domain, a task, a method, a track record, and systems of
> correction.

## Domain is more precise than subject

Two specialists may work under the same broad subject while answering
different questions. A climate modeler, field ecologist, historian of science,
and energy economist can all speak about climate while possessing distinct
competence.

Responsible deference begins by matching the claim to the actual subdomain and
task. Does the question require measurement, causal explanation, prediction,
interpretation, design, or ethical judgment? Skill in one does not automatically
license authority in another.

## Credentials are proxies

Degrees, positions, publications, awards, and institutional affiliations are
useful because direct evaluation of competence is costly. They are proxies,
not magic seals.

A good proxy is connected to demonstrated training and correction. A weak
proxy may reflect status, access, or reputation without current performance.
Rejecting all credentials is foolish; worshipping them is equally lazy.

## Experts disagree

Disagreement does not show that expertise is meaningless. It may indicate an
immature field, different assumptions, distinct questions, sparse evidence,
value-sensitive thresholds, or ordinary error.

The layperson should not respond by choosing the expert whose conclusion feels
best. Inspect the structure of disagreement:

- Is it inside the expert's domain?
- Do the parties share the same evidence?
- Are they disagreeing about facts, models, or values?
- Is one position marginal because it is neglected, or because it repeatedly
  fails?
- What would move each side?

## Institutions can outperform persons

The epistemic unit is often not an isolated expert but a corrective network:
training, peer criticism, replication, standards, records, competition, and
appeal. Institutions can preserve knowledge beyond individual memory and
expose error through organized disagreement.

They can also fail through shared incentives, gatekeeping, dependence, and
suppression. Institutional trust should therefore attach to visible correction
mechanisms, not merely to authority.

## Deference can be responsible

Responsible deference does not require pretending to understand the entire
technical basis of a conclusion. It requires understanding why this source is
better positioned than oneself, what the source is actually claiming, how
uncertain it is, and what checking structures surround it.

The opposite of blind obedience is not universal self-appointment. It is
audited dependence.

## Experts are often translators of uncertainty

Public audiences do not merely need a specialist's conclusion. They need the
shape of the specialist's uncertainty. Which result is stable? Which depends
on a model? Which disagreement changes the recommended action? Which is
technically alive but practically remote?

Communication fails when experts export either too much machinery or too
little structure. The first produces a wall of terms. The second produces a
command. A responsible expert compresses without pretending that compression
was lossless.

The hearer has obligations too. Demanding absolute certainty from a field that
works in probabilities is not rigor. It is a method for dismissing every
unwelcome conclusion. Yet accepting every confident summary is merely the same
laziness with better manners.

## The adjacent-domain temptation

Expertise produces habits of success, and success encourages travel. A person
who has mastered one difficult field may reasonably possess general analytical
strength. That strength is not the missing local evidence, tacit practice, or
error history of another domain.

The dangerous expert is not always the fraud. It is often the genuine expert
who has walked three rooms beyond his instruments while the audience continues
to applaud the white coat.

## An expertise audit

1. What exact claim and task require expertise?
2. What is the smallest relevant domain?
3. What evidence shows competence in that domain?
4. Which correction mechanisms constrain the expert?
5. Does disagreement concern fact, model, threshold, or value?
6. Is prestige leaking beyond competence?
7. What would count as failure for this expert judgment?

Expert communities often speak with substantial agreement. Such agreement can
be powerful evidence, but only after we ask how it was produced.
