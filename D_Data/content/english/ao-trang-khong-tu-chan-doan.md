A doctor, a cook, and a plumber stand beside a blocked toilet.

The coat does not choose who is right.

The doctor may read signs of illness. The plumber may find the blocked pipe.
The cook may know what entered the meal. Each can be useful. None becomes lord
of the whole room.

Skill belongs to a job, a method, and a range. A heart doctor may know little
about bridges. A famous cook may be a poor doctor. Even inside the right job,
people differ in care, records, tools, and willingness to be checked.

Suppose the cook says the drain is blocked by grease. The plumber opens the
pipe and finds a toy. The cook was speaking inside a nearby field, not the
right one. Suppose the plumber then tells the doctor what caused your fever.
Winning one question did not crown him king of all questions.

Real skill can show its work. It knows the common failures of its tools. It can
say when a case falls near the edge of its experience. It makes room for a
second check when the price of error is high.

This is why “an expert said it” is too little, while “experts know nothing” is
even worse. Ask: expert in what, using what, judging which part, with what
record, and under what checks?

Good authority is not magic stored in a person. It is a working relation
between skill and the problem in front of us.

Respect the coat when it fits the task. Look past it when it does not.
