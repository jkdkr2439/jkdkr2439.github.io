<p>Someone asked me: if you read so few books, where do you find the material to write?</p>

<p>The question is revealing because it assumes that books are where material originates. More often, a book is where reality has already been gathered, selected, processed, arranged, and bound by somebody else. I do not deny the value of books. It is simply that, for nearly thirty years, the thing I read most was never kept on a shelf. It passed before me, sat beside me on a park bench, argued in the alley, bled on the road, became trapped in a public toilet with no paper, and vanished before anyone gave it a name.</p>

<p>I read the world before it was edited.</p>

<h2>1. The roofless library</h2>

<p>I have sat on park benches listening to birds pass through the noise of traffic. Leaves did not lecture me on biology, weather, or acoustics. They simply moved. Yet if one watches long enough, one notices that wind does not appear directly. We know it through what it causes other things to do. An invisible phenomenon declares itself through the reactions of another body.</p>

<p>That is already a lesson in epistemology, even before anyone names it.</p>

<p>I have watched people from the roadside: a hurried man repeatedly looking behind him, a well-dressed woman grimacing when a street vendor approaches, a parent shouting at a child and immediately softening his voice when his superior calls. People describe themselves in language. Bodies, pauses, glances, and the voices they reserve for power often tell another account.</p>

<p>When a child selling lottery tickets passes, I do not see only a child selling tickets. Questions branch: when did the child wake, who supplied the tickets, who absorbs a shortage, is there school, which adult stands behind the trade, how has the child learned to classify faces, where will the child sleep? These possibilities are not facts. They identify the information still missing.</p>

<p>Honest curiosity does not invent another person's story. It hears the questions an appearance has not answered.</p>

<h2>2. Things too crude to be called knowledge</h2>

<p>Life does not teach only through sunsets and cinematic faces. It teaches through sewage, stomach pain, a broken sandal, an empty phone battery, and a public toilet with no paper.</p>

<p>At such moments, elegant abstractions retreat. The body immediately reorganizes value. A roll of paper can matter more than a speech. A door that locks can matter more than a monument. Civilization is not only museums and constitutions. It is also whether a stranger can satisfy a basic need without humiliation.</p>

<p>Hunger teaches resources. Pain teaches limits. Waiting teaches that time is distributed unequally. Rain without shelter teaches architecture. A rent increase teaches ownership. A pharmacy counter and insufficient money teach how a price can become a verdict.</p>

<p>These lessons have no table of contents. They arrive with temperature, odor, noise, shame, and consequence. They are dismissed as too ordinary to be knowledge, although most systems of thought eventually return to the task of handling ordinary things.</p>

<h2>3. Pages written directly on the nervous system</h2>

<p>I once watched a dog cut in half by a train in an instant. There was no music, narration, or pause for preparation. A living thing was whole, and then it was not.</p>

<p>I have been struck until my head bled. I have survived accidents and approached the border of death more than once. None of this makes me noble or automatically wise. Pain grants no diploma in wisdom. Many people suffer and learn only how to make others suffer.</p>

<p>But an intense event possesses what its description cannot: it writes simultaneously upon body, sensation, memory, reflex, and prediction. One can forget a definition while still ducking at the sound of something passing overhead. One may never recount an accident, while the body silently changes how it crosses a road.</p>

<p>An event ending in the world does not mean it has ended in a person.</p>

<p>Those images remain. They do not rest like objects in storage. They reactivate when a corresponding signal arrives: metal grinding on a rail, blood on another face, brakes compressing time. Each return restores the past while allowing the present to write upon it again.</p>

<p>I did not merely undergo these things. I continue to live with what they left behind.</p>

<h2>4. Experience is not truth</h2>

<p>Going outside does not guarantee accurate sight. Experience is data, not conclusion. A person deceived once may conclude that humanity is dishonest. A person helped once may decide that goodness always triumphs. Reality supplied genuine data, but the mind still built a false model.</p>

<p>The question is not only how much one experiences, but how one processes experience.</p>

<p>I preserve a scene, open several explanations, construct an argument and generate its opposition. I change the observing position, extend the time axis, ask what preceded it, what may follow, who receives benefit, who pays cost, which parts are evidence, and which remain conjecture. Contradictory accounts may each be valid within different domains. They may clash only because one word is being used for two mechanisms.</p>

<p>Experience supplies material. Recursive thought turns material into structure.</p>

<p>Without that discipline, travel merely enlarges the warehouse of prejudice. One can live sixty years while repeating the same year sixty times.</p>

<h2>5. Books and the world are not enemies</h2>

<p>For nearly thirty years I read few books. What I did read often included comics, manga, and manhwa, forms dismissed by people who enjoy policing intellectual legitimacy. Yet comics teach how a frame holds time, how blank space produces rhythm, how a minimally drawn face carries tragedy, and how power can appear in composition before a character speaks.</p>

<p>I am not against books. I simply refuse to grant them a monopoly on producing thought.</p>

<p>Books offer lives we cannot live, eras we cannot revisit, and experiments we lack the apparatus to perform. But a book is also a map drawn by someone else, with the scale, blind spots, purpose, and boundaries of its maker. Reading without collision with life makes it easy to confuse the coherence of sentences with the coherence of reality.</p>

<p>Experience without inherited structure has the opposite danger: spending a lifetime rediscovering a wheel that has turned for millennia.</p>

<p>A good book does not replace the world. It adds a way of reading it. The world does not refute books. It tests whether their ideas can remain alive outside the page.</p>

<h2>6. Material is everywhere</h2>

<p>I learned power by watching a person change voice before someone who controlled his livelihood. I learned economics from a vendor counting every small note while institutions misplaced numbers too large to imagine. I learned morality when people invoked goodness to humiliate someone publicly. I learned love when two people claimed to care for each other while each definition of care became a different prison.</p>

<p>Queues teach systems when one intruder forces everyone behind to reorganize. A badly hung door teaches design. The distance between what is reasonable and what is legal teaches law. Certainty defeated by consequence teaches probability. A faint smell reopening a vanished room teaches memory.</p>

<p>Even boredom is data. It reveals what attention is hungry for. Awkward conversation reveals where two systems of definition fail to align. Misunderstanding shows what language dropped in transmission. A quarrel between neighbors may contain economics, ownership, gender, honor, family history, and a miniature regime of power within a few square meters.</p>

<p>Material has never been scarce. What is scarce is attention sustained long enough for a phenomenon to stop being scenery and begin revealing its mechanism.</p>

<h2>7. This is how I have read for thirty years</h2>

<p>If you ask how someone who reads few books still finds material, the answer is that I never stopped reading.</p>

<p>I read things not recognized as texts: the sound of a room before a lie, the crowd turning from an inconvenient fact, calloused hands, worn shoes, uneven meals, kindness without cameras, evil convinced of its righteousness, the body in pain, pride wounded, fear disguised as anger.</p>

<p>I also read what I misunderstood. That is often the most valuable part. A detected error gives more than another answer. It exposes the machine inside us that manufactured the wrong one.</p>

<p>Those thirty years do not form a silent library. They form a moving field of signals. Old scenes meet new data, change position, connect, resist one another, and open further questions. What was once a wound can become an instrument of measure. What was once truth can become evidence of the observer's former limit.</p>

<p>I do not remember in order to worship the past. I remember so that the past can remain on trial.</p>

<h2>8. Reality needs no license to become knowledge</h2>

<p>Human beings enjoy issuing legitimacy. An activity must happen in school to be called learning. A discovery must appear in a laboratory to be called science. A thought must wear a scholar's name to be called philosophy. It is as if phenomena were not permitted to exist before terminology.</p>

<p>Yet a child tests aerodynamics with a paper plane before learning the word. A vendor models behavior by remembering which customers bargain. A mother runs thousands of risk predictions around a child before probability is written as notation. The name arrives later. The mechanism was already alive.</p>

<p>Knowledge is not born when an institution stamps it. The stamp helps a community store, test, and transmit claims by common standards. That is crucial. But validation should not be confused with the authority to create reality.</p>

<p>I read few books. That is true.</p>

<p>But I read a dog losing wholeness in an instant. I read blood from my own head. I read accidents revealing that the distance between continuation and ending may be one reflex. I read the child selling tickets, the man changing voice, the leaf declaring wind, the broken door, the absent paper, and thousands of lives too small for history to record.</p>

<p>A book is the world someone has already written into sentences.</p>

<p>For most of my life, I have read the world while it was still happening.</p>
