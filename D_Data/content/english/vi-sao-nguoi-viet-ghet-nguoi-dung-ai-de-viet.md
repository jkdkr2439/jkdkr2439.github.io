<header><p>TIỂU LUẬN</p></header>

<p>Or: excrement, the scarcity of language, and the repricing of intelligence</p>

<p>To say that writers hate AI is not quite accurate. Many writers use it, usually at night, with the discretion society once reserved for adultery and now reserves for asking ChatGPT a question and later calling the result “brainstorming.” What unsettles some of them is not that a machine can produce a beautiful sentence. Machines have long made many things more beautiful than human beings do, and have had the good manners not to demand their names on the cover. The real offence is that someone who never belonged to the literary guild may spend an afternoon with AI and emerge with prose that is not merely smoother, but appears to think better. Language used to be a fence around intelligence; now the fence is sold by monthly subscription. Those inside the garden naturally call this a moral crisis. Those outside generally call it a door.</p>

<p>This essay will explain the matter by means of a piece of excrement. Not because excrement is extraordinary, but because it is ordinary enough to lack the protection of a philosophical halo. If you wish to discover what a person does to the world, do not begin by giving him war, love, freedom, or existence. Those nouns arrive already dressed for dinner; anyone standing beside them looks profound. Give him a turd instead: an object incapable of flattering an intellectual, unwilling to become beautiful merely because it has been cited, and, most importantly, unable to protest when made to carry the entire destiny of mankind.</p>

<p>One afternoon, in a café for writers—where a latte cost eighty-five thousand đồng but the toilet hose had remained broken for three months—three men entered the same lavatory in succession. A previous visitor had left a fairly complete production in the bowl and forgotten to flush. The first man was called Dính Phân. I am not suggesting that the name ought to remind anyone of “Lính Tuân,” nor that a mischievous mind should proceed from there to invent a certain “Tuấn L<em>*</em>.” If such an indecent little genealogy has already formed in your imagination, it arrived without an invitation from me. An author is responsible for what he writes; a reader, occasionally, must accept the far graver responsibility of what he enjoys understanding. I remain entirely innocent—which is the most elegant position from which to arrange the evidence. Dính Phân was, of course, fictional. Any resemblance to the living, the dead, or anyone currently publishing on Substack merely proves that humanity has invented very few ways of standing before a turd. Dính Phân looked down. He did not flush. He reflected. Why do human beings recoil from something that, only hours before, had been peacefully contained within them? Why is matter entirely acceptable on one side of the sphincter and unclean a few centimetres beyond that biological frontier? Might the whole history of civilisation rest upon the same manoeuvre: expel a part of oneself, then declare it inferior? The turd began to look anxious. Dính Phân began to acquire a thesis.</p>

<p>Perhaps, he thought, the classes abandoned by society resemble waste. Perhaps disgust is an instrument of power. Perhaps cleanliness is merely the ruling body imposing its preferences upon bodies that refuse obedience. Ten minutes later, Eric Hoffer, Schopenhauer, John Stuart Mill, and several men dead since the nineteenth century had been dragged into the lavatory without their consent. Scholarship has the virtue of making any object serious, and the defect of occasionally making one forget that the object must still be flushed. Dính Phân emerged wearing the expression of a man who had just solved a problem of civilisation. The turd remained where it was.</p>

<p>The second man entered. His name was Tần Chiến. He looked down as well, but at that instant a last ray of afternoon light passed through the vent and settled upon the water. He suddenly remembered a lavatory in Vienna in 1912. No one knew why; the loveliest associations are rarely asked to show a passport. Vienna led him to Freud; Freud to a Japanese purification ritual; Japan to Heidegger; Heidegger to memory; memory to time; and time back to the body, like a gentleman travelling around the world merely to avoid admitting that he had forgotten to flush. Two thousand eight hundred words later, the turd had become a question: does what we discard truly remain behind, or does the act of leaving it become part of what we carry into the future? Tần Chiến stood outside the toilet and gazed into the distance. Perhaps, he thought, the turd never left us. We left the turd. Very beautiful. The turd remained where it was.</p>

<p>The two writers had rescued the object from vulgarity by opposite routes. Dính Phân gave it a historical indictment; Tần Chiến gave it a poetic passport. Art often confers dignity upon a thing by refusing to let it remain itself. This is not necessarily wicked. The world possesses a public reputation and private machinery, and literature is exceptionally gifted at tending the reputation. Then the third man entered with the disagreeable habit of wanting to open the machine. He looked into the bowl. “Oh. Yellow stool.” He took out his phone, not to make an artistic photograph. “What did this fellow eat yesterday?” That was when the story began.</p>

<p>Faced with one phenomenon, the three men did not see one thing. Dính Phân asked what it said <em>about humanity</em>: dignity, abasement, power, liberty, slavery, civilisation, the crowd. Tần Chiến asked what it <em>resembled and could be connected to</em>: history, memory, architecture, ritual, time, and a Viennese lavatory that may never have existed but sounded intensely inward. The third man asked a less elegant question: <em>what made it become this way?</em> Why yellow? Why loose? Why was it firm yesterday and less pungent last week? Where did the water enter, where did the pigment come from, what food was passing through, how had intestinal transit changed, and where did bacteria participate? Medication, stress, diet, bile, absorption, time—which variable changed when the output changed? To him, the turd was not a symbol. It was the output of a system.</p>

<p>The distinction is visually small and epistemically enormous. We meet the world first at its surface: colour, smell, texture—if life has carried the hand rather farther than originally intended—disgust, beauty, comfort, recollection, and names. Yet the senses encounter only the way a phenomenon appears before an observer; the mechanism producing it generally remains out of sight. We see yellow stool but not the chain that made it yellow. We see a man’s anger but not the history of signals, hormones, memories, incentives, social relations, and material conditions condensed into it. We see a bankrupt company but not the thousand earlier allocations of resources; a sick society but not the feedback loops that nourished the disease. One person asks, “What does it mean?” Another asks, “What is it like?” A third asks, “What made it become this?” All three questions can be useful. Wisdom begins by giving each its proper jurisdiction, for a metaphor does not fail because it cannot cure an illness; it fails only when it applies for the post of diagnosis.</p>

<p>Then AI arrived and performed a very impolite service: it removed part of the scarcity that had protected literary prestige. In the old days, if Dính Phân wished to drag twenty philosophers into a lavatory, he at least had to know on which shelves they were kept. If Tần Chiến wished to begin in a seventeenth-century London coffeehouse, pass through an economist, a German philosopher, and a neurological study, then conclude at a Japanese temple, he needed a sufficiently large store of anecdotes to build that bridge without falling into the water. An ordinary reader knew ten stories; he knew a thousand. The reader remembered two philosophers; he remembered twenty. The reader saw a turd; he saw Nietzsche standing behind it. That scarcity was real, and everything that derives its price from scarcity is offended when the supply increases.</p>

<p>One day Dính Phân opened ChatGPT. “Give me twenty philosophers, sociologists, and psychologists who have discussed disgust, pollution, the body, exclusion, and dignity.” AI said, “Certainly.” Dính Phân fell silent. Tần Chiến opened ChatGPT. “Give me fifteen historical anecdotes involving lavatories, waste, memory, disappearance, and time. Prefer opening hooks, then suggest bridges to Heidegger, Mary Douglas, and predictive processing.” AI said, “Certainly.” Tần Chiến fell silent too. The third man opened ChatGPT. “Yellow loose stool for two days. Group candidate mechanisms under diet, transit time, bile metabolism, fat absorption, medication, and infection. State which observations would help eliminate each hypothesis.” AI said, “Certainly.” He looked at the screen. “Bloody marvellous.” The same instrument; three entirely different experiences. The machine does not merely answer. It inherits the manners of the question placed before it. A question fond of jewellery turns it into a jeweller; a question fond of mechanisms turns it into a scouting party. AI seldom cures a user’s method. It first magnifies it.</p>

<p>This is why saying that writers hate AI is insufficient. What may disturb them more is the <em>AI user</em>: someone buffed by an immense store of data who produces work displaying a form of thought the literary class once treated as the privilege of those who were “deep,” “soulful,” “well read,” or “well lived.” A machine that composes a beautiful sentence is annoying; a non-writer who uses one to ask a better question is an assault upon the private ranking of intelligence people carry in their heads. Many have grown accustomed to treating verbal ability as a receipt for thought: I write deeply, therefore I think deeply; I possess many metaphors, therefore I perceive many layers of reality; I can introduce Foucault, Nietzsche, Freud, and a wild-haired German into a single paragraph, therefore something formidable must be taking place in my mind. Not necessarily. One person may understand a great deal and write like a washing-machine manual. Another may understand very little and write so movingly about the loneliness of a sock lost in the machine that you weep. Both abilities are real. They are simply not the same ability.</p>

<p>AI makes expression cheap. Metaphors become cheaper; sentence structures, anecdotes, quotations, conceptual associations, and voices become cheaper. Even the opening of a French novelist abandoned by his lover at a railway station can now be delivered in seconds, postage included. None of these things becomes worthless; their supply merely becomes enormous. Imagine a cook in a village where only he knows how to make mayonnaise. Each time he beats egg yolk with oil, the villagers gather in a circle. “Oh. Genius. Soul. Those hands must have suffered.” One day an automatic mayonnaise machine appears for three hundred thousand đồng. The cook now has two choices: learn to use it and prepare a more difficult dish, or establish a movement for the sacred protection of egg yolk. Writers face the same decision. When a tool democratises technique, a guild often renames its former privilege “tradition.” Tradition is the polite name a competitive advantage adopts when it begins to panic.</p>

<p>The mechanism hunter had a different bottleneck. He did not lack a beautiful sentence; he lacked data, vocabulary, analogous cases from other disciplines, time to read a hundred papers, the seventeenth candidate mechanism, and awareness of the variable he had missed. AI resembles a speaking library, or a team of junior analysts who may be wrong but can search the warehouse at speed. He must still choose the question, decide what deserves testing, notice when the model has joined unrelated things, and build the graph himself. But his search space suddenly expands. Yesterday he could imagine five hypotheses; today the machine offers fifty. His task is no longer to suffer in pursuit of a sixth, but to kill forty-five stupid ones. This is a different kind of leverage. The tool does not replace intelligence. It changes where intelligence must work.</p>

<p>One afternoon a motorbike died in the road. Dính Phân regarded it. “Perhaps the impotence of modern man before the machine he created is the deepest tragedy of technocracy.” Tần Chiến stood beside him. “In 1885, when Gottlieb Daimler…” A mechanic passing by opened the fuel tank. “There’s no bloody petrol in it.” The two writers had travelled from modern crisis to technological alienation in two thousand four hundred words. The mechanic required eight seconds. This does not make him more profound than Heidegger. It merely reminds us that some problems do not need more meaning; they need the correct mechanism. A patient says his stool is black. A poet may answer, “Black as the region the body hides from consciousness.” Beautiful. A doctor asks, “Are you taking iron? Do you have abdominal pain?” Suddenly colour ceases to be an aesthetic property and becomes data; an output is used to infer the system upstream. One person describes the output, another assigns meaning to it, and a third asks which prior states could have produced it. The third operation is reverse engineering, and AI is unusually suited to the game.</p>

<p>At this point a writer may reasonably object that literature was never employed to explain bilirubin. Quite right. No one asks poets to conduct stool tests, and a world in which every poem carried a confidence interval would be a peer-reviewed hell. A poet may look at excrement and write beautifully about mortality; a novelist may turn it into a motif across a character’s life; an essayist may use it as an analogy that makes a difficult concept clear. These are genuine abilities. None, however, automatically grants the right to claim an understanding of why stool is yellow. Likewise, writing beautifully about society does not entail understanding how society operates; describing pain accurately does not reveal the mechanism of pain; naming dozens of scholars does not guarantee a better causal model than that of a less verbal person who knows which node connects to which. Literature is not diminished by the limits of its jurisdiction. Only vanity considers a boundary an insult.</p>

<p>In the past, excellent expression was scarce enough to be mistaken for excellent thought. Today an ordinary prose writer can spend an afternoon with a model and produce something clean, coherent, rich in analogy, and even stylish. The surface can no longer perform classification so easily. Readers must ask less convenient questions. What new question has this person asked? What relation has he discovered? Which things formerly grouped together has he distinguished? What does his model predict? If A changes, how does he expect B to change? Can the method travel to another domain? Or could one replace the turd with love, loneliness, nationalism, or a Japanese temple and leave the entire storytelling engine untouched? This is where the identity threat appears. If your professional identity is, “I use language in a way others cannot,” AI applies pressure. If it is, “I see something others have not seen, and language is how I carry it outward,” AI may be excellent news. It does not take away thought; it reduces friction between thought and expression. It does not give you something to say. It merely makes it harder to conceal that you have nothing.</p>

<p>The apparent conflict is therefore writer versus AI, but beneath it lies a repricing. <strong>When language becomes cheap, whatever stands before language becomes expensive:</strong> questions, models, mechanisms, distinctions, discoveries, structures of inference, the ability to detect error, and the ability to construct a view the model was never asked to construct. A person who owns only a style sees AI as a rival. A thinker who used to express himself slowly may experience it as the removal of chains. A scientist sees an assistant, a coder a pair programmer, an entrepreneur leverage, while a mechanism hunter asks, “How many more nodes can this thing open for me?” Some writers do not hate the machine. They hate the sight of an outsider using it to cross the professional fence—and worse, discovering that the outsider can not only write, but think differently. Intelligence has always praised freedom in speeches and disliked it in markets.</p>

<p>On another day, Dính Phân, Tần Chiến, and the third man met again in the lavatory. The turd was still there. Dính Phân was explaining that society’s attitude toward waste reflected the power relation between honoured subjects and excluded existences. Tần Chiến had found a beautiful anecdote about the sewers of Rome and was preparing a bridge to collective memory. The third man bent over, studied the bowl, and asked its owner, “Did you eat turmeric yesterday?” “Yes.” “Ah.” Case closed. He flushed, then opened AI. “Incidentally, by what mechanisms can curcumin affect stool colour?” Outside the lavatory, the two writers continued debating whether AI possessed a soul. The turd, at last, had been released from the obligation to carry the entire destiny of mankind.</p>

<p>Some people look at the world for more things to say <em>about</em> it. Others look to discover <em>why it became what it is</em>. AI is extremely good at helping both. Yet the cheaper a tool makes speech, the more accurately it forces us to pay for understanding. And sometimes, before a civilisation overflowing with magnificent answers, the most intelligent question remains the one the turd had been patiently awaiting from the beginning: why is it yellow?</p>
