<section id="section-1"><h2>1. A civilization reduced to a recruitment desk</h2><p>There is a modest employment office inside certain people's heads. It has no windows, one counter, and an official authorized to pronounce on the usefulness of everyone outside. Present an unfamiliar project, an unconventional livelihood or an ambition not yet yielding a salary, and the official produces the same instruction: get a job. The economy has apparently finished evolving. Its final achievement is somebody else's permission to put your name on a payroll. Everything beyond the counter is either unemployment or an elaborate attempt to conceal it.</p><p>An invitation to find paid work can, of course, be sensible. Rent does not accept conceptual originality as payment. Somebody who is living on other people's labor while refusing every available responsibility may need an inconveniently practical conversation. My subject is not that conversation. It is the moment a practical suggestion becomes a complete theory of human usefulness. Nor am I claiming that Gen Z, or the children grouped under Gen Alpha, share a single economic imagination. The target is a habit of judgment, whoever performs it. A young person does not become a representative sample merely by being irritating online.</p><p>Beside this contemporary instruction I want to place an older formulation. In section 5 of Đường Kách mệnh, published in 1927 under the name Nguyễn Ái Quốc, the text classifies capitalists with the words “Tư bản (không làm công mà hưởng lợi).” The following example contrasts the toil of Hòn Gay miners with an owner described as taking no part in the work while receiving substantial gains. It would therefore be too narrow to paraphrase this as merely not being a wage employee. The formulation belongs to an argument about classes and oppression. My question concerns what happens when that condensed classification is applied to every owner without examining the activity and relations in each case.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup></p><p>I am not proposing that a revolutionary text and a social-media taunt are politically equivalent. They are not. I am asking what happens when people stop knowing what their words are doing. One category turns every owner into the same moral creature; another allows an employer to certify whether a life has economic meaning. Both can make a large world fit into an astonishingly small room. The task is not to choose which room deserves our obedience. It is to recover the doors, the missing terrain and the capacity to redraw the plan.</p></section>

<section id="section-2"><h2>2. Return a definition to the time in which it worked</h2><p>The Revolutionary Path announces a revolutionary purpose and presents organization, class relations and collective action as practical questions. Its language distinguishes positions in a struggle and links the liberation of oppressed people to a wider revolutionary project. We can read its compressed formulations in relation to political education and mobilization, but that context does not cancel their explicit content. The text itself makes the broad classification: it is not merely something later readers invented. If the distinction is insufficient for analyzing entrepreneurial, technical or managerial work, that is a limitation to examine directly. Neither quietly narrowing its meaning nor stripping away its theoretical commitments would be an honest way to recruit the author into our present argument.<sup><a href="#source-1">1</a></sup></p><p>A definition is sometimes designed to interrupt an arrangement that people have learned to endure. Imagine workers who can describe their exhaustion individually but cannot yet name the distribution of control around them. One suffers in a workshop, another on a plantation, another under the demands of creditors. A political vocabulary can connect these injuries, identify positions and make organization thinkable. That is a different assignment from distinguishing the compensation of a salaried manager from the return to an absent shareholder. A tool can be forceful at one task and incomplete at another without either fact being a scandal.</p><p>This does not make historical context a detergent that washes every proposition clean. A statement can be contested in its own time, and a political simplification can hide differences that matter. We still have to ask whether the distinctions work, whom they illuminate and whom they erase. Context tells us what is being attempted; it does not relieve the attempt of scrutiny. Conversely, discovering a limitation in an old formulation does not entitle us to write a flattering novel about our superiority to people confronting a situation we have never had to survive.</p><p>The least interesting descendant of a thinker is not the person who remembers a sentence imperfectly. It is the person who remembers it perfectly while forgetting the work it was meant to perform. A sign erected at a dangerous crossing becomes an ancestral monument. The road is widened, the traffic changes, another neighborhood appears, but someone continues directing everyone toward the old ditch in the name of fidelity. Such loyalty preserves the paint at the expense of the passengers. Respecting a historical instrument requires more than refusing to touch it. It requires knowing when it is pointing at something that is no longer there.</p></section>

<section id="section-3"><h2>3. Respect is not permission to speak for the dead</h2><p>We need not invent what Hồ Chí Minh would say in 2026 to demonstrate that his political language cannot be exhausted by one extracted formulation. His letter to Vietnamese industrial and commercial circles, published on 13 October 1945, encouraged their participation in economic construction and connected their activity to the prosperity of the country. It addressed a different political situation and a different audience from the revolutionary lessons associated with 1927. That is a documented difference of address and purpose, not a séance conducted by an enthusiastic admirer.<sup><a href="#source-2">2</a></sup></p><p>The letter does not make him an advocate of unrestricted private power. Public benefit remains central to the appeal. Neither does a call for economic construction erase class analysis or resolve every later question of ownership. What it prevents is a particularly lazy reading: the claim that one hostile description of capitalists licenses treating every business activity, across every circumstance, as the same political object. A historical person can distinguish tasks and alliances. The person who turns him into a single reusable caption is often displaying less confidence in his thought than in the convenience of the caption.</p><p>My own respect for a historical figure does not require granting him advance authority over every problem that will arise after his death. Political organization is not molecular biology; courage is not an accounting method; a contribution to national independence is not a warranty on every sentence. If we make respect depend on universal infallibility, we build a fragile altar. The first necessary correction then feels like demolition. There is another possibility: recognize what a person did, examine what he argued, distinguish the circumstances, and remain responsible for the conclusions we ourselves choose to draw.</p><p>The same restraint applies to critics. It is possible to use a historical figure as a permanent defendant just as mechanically as others use him as a permanent witness for the prosecution. In either case, the living avoid part of their work. They ask a name to settle a question before specifying its terms. I would rather put the name back into history and bring the question into the present. Who now controls the resources? Who makes decisions? Who can refuse? What does the arrangement produce, and who carries its costs? An old text can help us begin that inquiry. It cannot perform all its contemporary observations for us.</p></section>

<section id="section-4"><h2>4. A definition needs a job description</h2><p>When I use a concept, I want to know at least three things: which phenomenon it selects, which relation it makes visible, and what decision the distinction is supposed to improve. These are working questions, not a magical rule that three words can settle any dispute. Consider the word owner. In a question about legal title, it identifies one kind of relation. In a question about daily production, it may identify very little about who actually organizes the work. In a question about family responsibility, it may be almost irrelevant. The word has not become false. We have changed what we are asking it to do.</p><p>A person who owns a workshop may also design its tools, negotiate orders, sweep the floor and care for an ill parent. These roles do not cancel one another. If I call that person an owner, I have not explained the design work. If I call the person a hardworking designer, I have not explained the power conferred by ownership. What matters is being able to move between the relations without smuggling the conclusion from one into another. A useful map retains the identity of the person while allowing the relevant coordinates to change.</p><p>This is not an invitation to make words mean whatever protects our preferred conclusion. A definition earns its place through clarity, consistency, contact with observable differences and usefulness under challenge. Suppose I define exploitation so narrowly that nobody I admire can commit it. The resulting innocence is manufactured by my dictionary. Suppose I define contribution so generously that every profitable act must benefit society. I have not defended the market. I have removed its possible failures from the vocabulary. The method must permit unwelcome cases to remain visible, including cases that make the author look less clever.</p><p>The problem is not that human beings use categories. Without compression we could scarcely finish a sentence, much less organize a life. The problem is forgotten compression. A label starts as an economical instruction for attention and ends as a substitute for attention. At that point, explanation becomes a small administrative ritual: identify the class, apply the adjective, close the file. The person has been understood without anybody having to investigate the arrangement. A remarkably efficient procedure, provided understanding was never the real objective.</p></section>

<section id="section-5"><h2>5. Capital, ownership and work do not name the same thing</h2><p>Capital, a capitalist, an entrepreneur and a manager are not interchangeable entries in a thesaurus. Depending on the analytical tradition, capital may refer to productive assets, finance, or a specific social process of value expansion. A capitalist is not simply anybody with an idea or a busy calendar. Ownership and the appropriation of returns matter to the classification. Entrepreneurship concerns the initiation and organization of activity under uncertainty; management concerns coordination and decisions. These functions can occupy one person, several people, or institutions whose ownership is distributed. The distinctions are useful precisely because real organizations do not respect the convenience of our favorite caricature.</p><p>A salaried executive can exercise substantial power without owning the enterprise. A shareholder can receive income without participating in its daily management. A craftsperson working alone can own tools and sell products without employing a wage-labor force. A cooperative can organize production through collective ownership. Even where the same individual supplies money, develops a product and directs a team, different parts of the eventual return may be attributed, debated or contracted differently. Calling everything the reward of the boss saves time only by erasing the question of what, exactly, is being rewarded.</p><p>For the purpose of this essay, I treat class position as a way of locating people in relations of ownership, control and dependence. It is not a complete moral biography. That does not make position harmless. A capacity to dismiss people, restrict access, set conditions or receive residual income changes the options available to others. Structural analysis is valuable because such powers do not disappear when the individual possessing them is charming. Conversely, the possession of a power does not by itself tell us every way that person will exercise it. We need both the structure and the conduct, not a competition over which one can abolish the other.</p><p>Imagine a business owner who develops an effective tool and then uses the firm's dominance to trap suppliers in punitive conditions. The invention does not retrospectively justify the trap. The trap does not make the invention cease to work. The same ledger may contain a genuine contribution and a genuine injury. People who demand that a single adjective settle both are usually defending something more comfortable than accuracy. Reality is under no obligation to distribute admirable work and ugly conduct among different people merely to make our judgments easier to print.</p></section>

<section id="section-6"><h2>6. Before arguing about exploitation, agree on what is being argued</h2><p>In Marx's account, exploitation is not merely a synonym for an employer being cruel, breaking a wage agreement or paying unusually little. In the first volume of Capital, surplus-value and the division between necessary and surplus labor are central to the account, including its measure of exploitation. Within that framework, an employer's personal diligence or the worker's agreement to a contract does not, by itself, remove the structural relation being analyzed. One can dispute the framework, but a refutation must address its premises and inferences. Discovering a polite employer does not constitute that refutation.<sup><a href="#source-3">3</a></sup></p><p>Everyday moral discussion often uses the same word differently: an arrangement takes unfair advantage of vulnerability, imposes disproportionate costs, or obstructs meaningful refusal. I will use that normative register when assessing particular conduct here. This is a declaration of scope, not a covert correction of Marx. It requires stated criteria. Withheld pay, deliberately concealed hazards, retaliation against reasonable objections, or a contrived inability to leave give us things to investigate. Whether a practice is also unlawful is a separate question requiring the applicable law and facts. A moral complaint does not become precise simply because its vocabulary sounds legally forceful.</p><p>The distinction blocks two easy performances. The first sees any return to ownership and assumes that nothing further needs examining. The second announces that investment has occurred and therefore everything afterward is deserved. Neither explains the production process, the alternatives available to workers, or the distribution of losses. Nor does a transaction's benefit to both parties prove fairness in every respect. A person may accept the least bad available choice and improve their position while remaining exposed to an avoidable abuse. Improvement relative to deprivation is important. It is not a universal certificate for the conditions of improvement.</p><p>There is a more demanding argument available. Identify the disputed practice, specify the standard, compare feasible alternatives and ask what mechanism reproduces the inequality. Is compensation being suppressed by genuine limits on revenue, by a temporary cash crisis, by unequal bargaining power or by a calculated exploitation of restricted mobility? Different answers imply different remedies. One might require better organization, another collective bargaining, another enforcement, another a change in ownership or market structure. A word that cannot survive these questions is functioning as an insult. A word that invites them can become an instrument.</p></section>

<section id="section-7"><h2>7. A wage has a history, and an exit has a price</h2><p>A wage is not a small numerical portrait of the worker's soul. It emerges within a relation involving demand, skill, productivity, competition, bargaining, information and the ability of each party to find an alternative. Even when output can be measured, the allocation of its proceeds does not mechanically announce itself. People whose tasks depend on one another create an additional difficulty: the enterprise's result cannot always be divided into isolated contributions without selecting a method and defending its assumptions. Arithmetic can be impeccable while the chosen allocation quietly does the political work.</p><p>Labor-market research gives a reason not to treat every employer as a passive recipient of a perfectly competitive wage. The scientific background to the 2021 economics prize discusses employer market power and explains why employment responses to minimum-wage changes depend on conditions. It cautions against the opposite universal conclusion as well. The narrow lesson I need is methodological: a clean verbal formula can fail when the structure of actual alternatives differs from its assumptions. It does not authorize importing a foreign estimate into every Vietnamese workplace.<sup><a href="#source-5">5</a></sup></p><p>Consider two hypothetical workers offered identical pay for identical hours. One has savings, nearby alternatives and family support. The other is supporting a dependent, would lose housing by moving, and cannot spend months looking. The printed contract might be the same; their capacity to decline is not. Now consider an employer who cannot replace a specialist without stopping a critical process. Bargaining power may shift the other way. These differences are not embarrassing exceptions to be swept from the model. They are part of the model we should have bothered to build.</p><p>I therefore distrust both the instant equation of low pay with abuse and the instant equation of a signature with fairness. Investigate what the pay can sustain, how it was set, what risks accompany it and what refusal would cost. Formal permission to leave matters, but the door can be open while the road outside is prohibitively expensive. Taking that cost seriously need not deny personal agency. It prevents us from praising freedom at exactly the level of abstraction where nobody has to finance its exercise.</p></section>

<section id="section-8"><h2>8. Profit is not a confession, or a certificate of innocence</h2><p>An employee usually encounters a firm after some of the work required to make it possible has already happened. Equipment has been acquired, a product tried, a customer found, a mistake paid for. The absence of those activities from today's factory floor does not mean they were imaginary. An owner may have invested years of technical work and endured unsuccessful attempts before the present operation became viable. Coordination can itself be labor: a correct sequence of deliveries may prevent skilled people from spending the day waiting beside an idle machine. Sweat is evidence of exertion, not a complete measure of contribution.</p><p>The word may is important. Some owners inherit functioning assets; some managers perform the organizing while an investor supplies funds; some fortunes depend on privileged access. Raising money can require persuasive competence and earned trust, but it can also benefit from family networks, social position or an inflated story that has not yet met its consequences. We must ask which case is before us. A motivational portrait of the founder who suffered in a garage does not establish the history of every company. Ownership is no more proof of past sacrifice than a scar is proof of having defended someone else.</p><p>Workers bring histories too. Training took time, transport costs money, illness does not disappear because it falls outside the firm's balance sheet. An investor may diversify financial exposure while an employee concentrates much of their income and bodily capacity in a single workplace. A small proprietor may face ruin; an employee may face a different, equally consequential loss. The relevant task is to trace the risks actually borne, the buffers available and the parties onto whom failure can be shifted. Reciting that one side takes risks while the other receives wages is not an analysis of risk. It is a seating plan for sympathy.</p><p>Profit indicates an accounting remainder under a particular system of costs and revenues. It may sustain replacement, expansion and a return that made investment worth attempting. Its existence alone establishes neither abuse nor social usefulness. A venture can be profitable because it solves a difficult problem, because it restricts competitors, because some costs are imposed on outsiders, or through a mixture of these conditions. Loss is not virtue either. A badly organized company can exhaust workers and capital simultaneously. The question is not whether money remains. It is what produced the remainder, what has been counted, and whose missing invoice made the result look attractive.</p></section>

<section id="section-9"><h2>9. What the firm organizes</h2><p>Suppose A needs a useful result, B, and another person can produce it. They may exchange directly. No corporation has to appear between them to confer economic legitimacy. But perhaps A needs dependable delivery, continuing maintenance, consistent quality and recourse when something goes wrong. The producer needs materials, information and a way to survive the interval before payment. These linked requirements can exceed what either person can conveniently arrange alone. Let D name an organization that coordinates them. The letters are only a simple illustration; D is not a scientific discovery hidden inside the alphabet.</p><p>Coase's 1937 question was why coordination takes place inside firms when markets also coordinate activity. His answer drew attention to the costs of discovering terms and repeatedly arranging transactions. Internal organization has costs too. This helps explain why neither one vast firm nor an unlimited succession of separate deals is automatically the best arrangement.<sup><a href="#source-4">4</a></sup></p><p>In our example, D may combine finance, labor, tools, schedules, records and customer commitments into a dependable process. A worker contributes more than a pair of hands: judgment on the production floor can improve the process itself. A manager need not personally invent that improvement to help it spread. A customer may supply information about a failure; an engineer changes the design; maintenance protects the change from being undone. The output becomes evidence for another cycle. This is a system of interdependent contributions, not a ceremonial parade in which everything important enters through the office of the owner.</p><p>A company is one possible architecture for that coordination. A cooperative, public institution, partnership or independent network may solve related problems under other distributions of authority and responsibility. Even a very small enterprise can be useful; size is not its defining achievement. We should ask what the arrangement allows people to accomplish, what it costs, and who can alter it. Remove the logos, job titles and inspirational wall lettering, and an organization must still make work possible. If its most impressive output is an explanation of why everyone beneath its leadership should be grateful, its process deserves a closer inspection.</p></section>

<section id="section-10"><h2>10. A rejection is not a theory of your existence</h2><p>An employment application can be understood as a proposed exchange. I can contribute a particular kind of work; your organization has a need and can offer compensation, tools, access or learning that I value. Do the terms fit? The formulation changes neither the employer's budget nor the applicant's immediate need. It changes where the applicant places authority. The company can decide whether to buy this work on these terms. That is already considerable power. It does not require the additional privilege of deciding whether the person possesses value in every other relation.</p><p>A refusal may mean that the role has changed, the price does not fit, the evidence of competence was unconvincing, or somebody else is better suited to this particular task. It may reveal a real limitation. It may also reflect incomplete information or a bad judgment. The applicant should not treat every rejection as proof of misunderstood genius. That would be the same error with the verdict reversed. What is needed is a diagnosis at the right scale. Which part of the proposed exchange failed, and what information would distinguish the possible explanations? A useful disappointment is specific enough to teach something.</p><p>Nor can one declare something economically valuable and oblige others to purchase it. A capacity needs a route toward a need, credible evidence, workable terms and delivery. The person who can build an elegant device but cannot establish whom it helps still has an unfinished problem. Yet an unfinished route is not the same as an empty person. A single employer is one possible partner in constructing that route, not the owner of every destination. Direct clients, collaborations, different industries and different forms of organization may reveal possibilities the original job description had no reason to include.</p><p>Even that larger market is not the final judge of a life. Care within a family, assistance to a neighbor, preservation of shared knowledge and work that prevents damage may have substantial usefulness without a buyer standing nearby. Price requires a particular arrangement of demand and ability to pay. Human worth does not await that arrangement. A criticism of the recruitment desk would be rather disappointing if it merely replaced the hiring manager with a cash register and declared the second machine more philosophically qualified to issue certificates of existence.</p></section>

<section id="section-11"><h2>11. Losing a job is not losing every capacity that made it possible</h2><p>The ordinary word unemployed should be allowed to describe somebody without paid employment. Nothing is gained by announcing that the person is not really unemployed because their skills survive. The loss of income can be immediate and frightening. My distinction is between a current position and the resources accumulated across a working life: skills, experience, relationships, evidence of completed work and an ability to learn. Vietnamese wordplay around nghề and nghiệp can invite the distinction, but it cannot serve as an etymological proof. Losing a place to practice a craft is not identical to losing the craft.</p><p>A programmer no longer on a company's payroll does not thereby forget how to program. A cook leaving a restaurant may retain considerable skill while losing access to its kitchen, ingredients and customers. A designer may have experience but lack permission to show confidential projects. These differences matter. What survives is neither nothing nor an intact business that can be carried away in a pocket. The useful inventory separates portable competence from borrowed infrastructure and from relationships that may or may not continue. It is more demanding than reassurance, and more hopeful than a verdict of personal extinction.</p><p>Accumulation is not guaranteed either. Knowledge can become less useful; exhaustion can reduce the ability to mobilize it; reputation may depend on an institution that no longer lends its name. A person can spend years performing a narrow task without receiving the chance to understand the wider process. These are reasons to examine the organization of work and learning, not to mock the worker for failing to construct a diversified personal enterprise after hours. Building continuity may require time and support that were never included in the motivational advice.</p><p>A career, in the sense I am proposing, is an evolving arrangement of capacities and commitments, not a ladder with ownership waiting at the summit. Someone can choose to become an excellent employee, an independent specialist, a teacher, an organizer or a founder. Changes between these forms need not be promotions or defeats. The important question is what the person is trying to sustain and what arrangement makes that possible. A life does not become directionless simply because its direction cannot be expressed as the name of the office it eventually hopes to occupy.</p></section>

<section id="section-12"><h2>12. Do not replace the cult of employment with the cult of the founder</h2><p>There is a familiar fraud in advice about autonomy: the speaker denounces dependence on an employer and immediately sells dependence on a new identity. Everyone must become an entrepreneur, cultivate a personal brand and convert every talent into a product. The map gains another road and promptly closes the old one. A person who wanted meaningful work, predictable hours and the ability to return home without carrying a business emergency has not failed to understand freedom. They may have selected a form of cooperation that suits their aims remarkably well.</p><p>Running an enterprise means solving a different set of problems. Finding demand, coordinating delivery, managing obligations and surviving uncertainty do not become enjoyable merely because the person performing them is called their own boss. The client, creditor, platform and cash cycle may supply plenty of supervision without issuing a staff badge. A worker can exercise substantial autonomy inside a well-designed organization. An independent seller can be highly dependent on one purchaser. Count the available decisions and alternatives before counting the titles. Freedom is not printed on the business card in a special font.</p><p>For somebody wishing to explore another route, a small, bounded test is often more informative than an identity declaration. A hypothetical cook might first investigate whether a specific group would buy a manageable service, whether access to a suitable kitchen is possible, and what obligations delivery would entail. The test may show that remaining employed while learning is the better choice. It may also reveal an opportunity. Neither result is a humiliation. The purpose is to obtain relevant information without casually staking the money needed for housing or care on an inspiring sentence about courage.</p><p>The right to choose is also collective. It may be increased by better public services, credible worker representation, accessible training, competitive markets and arrangements that do not punish every attempt to move. Personal initiative and institutional change are not enemies who must take turns explaining everything. One concerns what a person can do within a situation; the other concerns how that situation is made. Advice becomes cruel when it recognizes only the first. Political criticism becomes sterile when it refuses to acknowledge any room for action until the second has been perfected.</p></section>

<section id="section-13"><h2>13. Who has the power to change the terms?</h2><p>The historical proximity of capital, colonial rule and exploitation in Vietnamese political language should not be treated as an arbitrary vocabulary accident. Control of resources and coercive political power can reinforce one another. I use the phrase forced synchronization to describe an aspect of colonial integration: people become drawn into systems of production and exchange whose terms they have little power to set. It is an interpretive metaphor, not a claim that every colonial institution had one cause, or that technical differences made conquest either inevitable or legitimate. Guns do not become consent when they arrive with a railway timetable.</p><p>This perspective asks us to look beyond the mere appearance of an asset. A port can move goods; a railway can reduce a journey; a bank can organize credit. Those functions do not answer who financed them, who controlled access, whose purposes governed the investment and who could refuse the resulting arrangement. Nor should precolonial life be painted as a world without commerce, inequality or political coercion. Different arrangements must be investigated on their own terms. Replacing the fairy tale of benevolent conquest with a fairy tale of an innocent past leaves the method of producing fairy tales entirely undisturbed.</p><p>The contemporary relevance is not that every workplace is a colony. That analogy would erase crucial differences in sovereignty, violence and legal status. The relevant question is narrower and more portable: who can alter the terms of cooperation? A useful contribution can coexist with excessive control over access. A company may create a valuable product while preventing competitors from reaching customers; an intermediary may reduce transaction costs while becoming a bottleneck. Our evaluation must be capable of recognizing the initial service and its later transformation. Yesterday's solution does not purchase immunity from tomorrow's examination.</p><p>This also changes what it means to criticize institutions. Instead of asking only whether an owner is personally generous, ask whether those affected can obtain information, contest a decision, organize, leave or build an alternative. Instead of praising an abstract market, inspect whether the market is accessible. Instead of invoking national prosperity as an automatic defense of a firm, trace where benefits and costs actually go. A flag, like a balance sheet, can be made to stand in front of a question. Neither becomes an answer simply because people have learned to salute it.</p></section>

<section id="section-14"><h2>14. A larger map, not a more fashionable cage</h2><p>The common mechanism is now visible. A relation becomes a category; repetition conceals the relation; the category becomes a verdict. An owner receives income, so every owner is declared the same kind of person. Employment often provides a livelihood, so employment becomes the only recognized way to contribute. An employer rejects an application, so the rejected person is invited to regard the refusal as a total assessment. At each step, something contingent acquires a false permanence. The world has not become smaller. Our permission to notice it has.</p><p>Before accepting such a verdict, I would ask what the term selects, which comparison supports it, what is being omitted, who benefits from that omission and what evidence could make the conclusion change. These questions should also be directed at this essay. If my larger map overlooks people with few alternatives, it needs correcting. If my respect for entrepreneurial work excuses coercion, it has failed its own distinction. If criticism of a restrictive employment ideal becomes contempt for employees, the argument has walked out of one small room only to rent the one next door.</p><p>The point is not to spend a lifetime renegotiating definitions while other people get on with living. Good distinctions reduce wasted conflict and make decisions more concrete. An employee can investigate a skill gap without accepting a verdict of worthlessness. An owner can explain a real constraint without pretending every demand is unreasonable. A critic can distinguish an efficient process from an unjust allocation, and propose a remedy that addresses the thing being criticized. The advantage of a better map is not that it permits endless demonstrations of sophistication. It lets someone take a step that the old map made invisible.</p><p>Hồ Chí Minh does not need us to freeze our minds in the year of an extracted sentence in order to remain a significant historical figure. Marx does not need a caricature to make disagreement possible. Young people do not need to be flattened into a generational mascot for a bad joke. And workers do not need their livelihood mocked in order for alternatives to be imaginable. What we owe these different subjects is not identical approval, but the labor of asking a question adequate to each. That is more difficult than distributing reverence and contempt by category, and considerably more useful.</p><p>Get a job can be good advice about an immediate need. It becomes bad philosophy when it is used to close every other route between a person's capacities and the world. Seek work where work is needed; negotiate where negotiation is possible; organize when the terms require more than an individual can change. But do not mistake a recruitment desk for the border of economic reality. Before instructing an unfamiliar person to find an employer, it may be worth checking whether the thing missing from the conversation is not their usefulness, but a larger map in the mind of the person giving directions.</p></section>
