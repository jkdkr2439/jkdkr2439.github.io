The city began charging for shade during the hottest summer on record.

No one called it charging for shade. The project was named the **URBAN MICROCLIMATE REGULATION SYSTEM**, a title long enough to keep nature from recognizing its own property.

Smart canopies were installed in the square, outside the hospital, beside bus stops, and along the road that had once been lined with two rows of dầu trees. The trees had been cut down three years earlier to widen the street. At the time, the city explained that traffic required more space. The road was wider now, the traffic heavier, and pedestrians could buy back a patch of coolness with their phones.

Each canopy displayed a square code. Users scanned it, selected a number of minutes, and paid. A metal screen would unfold, slowly laying its shadow over a rectangle marked on the pavement.

The basic package included ten minutes.

The saver package included thirty.

The family package allowed up to four people to occupy the shade at once. Children under six were free with valid documentation.

Sunlight remained free.

This was regarded as an act of generosity.

On opening day, the deputy mayor stood beneath the largest canopy and delivered a speech on resilience. The climate was changing, he said, and the city had to innovate. Behind him was an architectural rendering of young people laughing in the shade, holding drinks with paper straws.

There were no trees in the picture.

Trees suffer from several defects: they grow slowly, shed leaves, crack pavements, and cannot submit revenue reports. Smart canopies were cleaner. They knew how many people they had served, at what hour, and for how long. They could refuse to operate until payment was received. Trees, lacking financial discipline, had once shaded even those without money.

During the first few weeks, residents used the canopies out of curiosity. Children liked watching shade slide out from the roof like a carpet. Adults took photographs. A married couple bought thirty minutes for lunch, then left after eighteen and regretted the remaining twelve as though they had wasted food.

The application did not allow unused minutes to be carried into the following day.

Shade was perishable.

At bus stop number nine, a delivery rider often stood outside the marked rectangle. He kept his helmet on, pulled his sleeves over his wrists, and waited through the red light. The canopy was half a step away. Those inside looked out; he looked at the shadow ending just before the toes of his shoes.

No one forbade him to enter. This was important. A civilized society rarely needs to forbid the poor from using what they cannot afford.

An old woman noticed him and moved aside.

“Come and stand in here.”

The rider shook his head.

“It counts people, Auntie.”

Her package covered only one person. The system had sensors. If it detected another body, the application would recommend an upgrade.

The old woman stepped out of the shade and stood beside him.

The canopy continued serving the empty piece of pavement until her ten minutes expired.

In that month's report, stop number nine achieved its target rate of operation according to design.

When the temperature climbed higher, the city introduced an assistance programme. People over seventy received a ten-percent discount during off-peak hours. Schoolchildren were awarded five free minutes if they watched an entire public-information video on energy conservation. Homeless residents could apply for a free code at the administrative centre, provided they supplied a permanent address.

The condition was revised after someone pointed out the inconvenience.

“Permanent address” was replaced with “proof of residence.”

The inconvenience remained, but under a different name.

Residents gradually adjusted the way they lived. In the morning they kept close to the walls of buildings. At noon they hurried across exposed stretches. In the afternoon they stood beneath billboards. The city became a map of evasions. People no longer asked which route was shortest, but which could be travelled without buying shade.

Cafés put umbrellas on the pavement, but the shadows belonged to customers. A guard was assigned to remind anyone borrowing them that the area formed part of the business premises. He always spoke quietly. Not from shame; people being moved along usually knew they were being moved along before the sentence reached them.

Children adapted fastest. They invented a game called Crossing the River. Sunlit squares were water; dark patches were stones. Anyone who stepped into the light died. Adults watched them leap from the shadow of a utility pole to the shadow of a rubbish bin and praised their imagination.

No one considered that imagination is sometimes merely a beautiful name for a survival skill learned too early.

In the second summer, the operating company introduced flexible pricing. Shade cost more at noon and less when the sky was cloudy. The pricing algorithm followed actual demand. It was the same principle the market applied to hotels, airline tickets, and now a place to stand without being burned by the sun.

One day, rain approached. Clouds sealed the city and the temperature fell. Every canopy reduced its price by eighty percent.

No one bought.

For half an hour, every street had shade. It spilled across the square, covered the marked rectangles, and lay even where no square code had been posted. People walked more slowly. The delivery rider removed his mask. The old woman from stop number nine could stand wherever she pleased.

Then the rain came.

The smart canopies folded themselves shut to protect their equipment.

People ran for shelter beneath doorways, surviving trees, and billboards. They grew wet while the system kept itself dry.

The company explained that this was a safety feature.

After the rain, a young flamboyant tree appeared in a crack near the square. No one knew where the seed had come from. A bird may have carried it, or it may have lain in the soil since before the road was widened.

A street cleaner meant to pull it out, but the rubbish truck arrived early that day and he forgot.

The tree lived.

It was small and cast no useful shade. Months later, someone tied a red cord around the stem to keep bicycles from running it down. The following year, its crown made a dark mark no larger than a hand.

No one noticed until a child placed one foot inside it.

The child stood perfectly still, solemn as an explorer who had discovered a territory.

His mother told him to come along. He asked:

“Do we have to pay for this bit?”

The mother looked around.

There was no code to scan.

No rectangle in which to stand.

No project name.

Not yet, she said.
