![A vein of gold in black stone](/assets/images/nhung-nguoi-ghet-vang/plate-01-discovery.png)

The gold ran through the black rock, thin as a thread caught beneath the skin.

I knelt in the mud to look at it. I still remember the feeling: my knees cold, both hands trembling, my heart beating as though someone had just reprieved me from a sentence I had not known I carried. I thought a joy that large could not be kept to oneself. I ran back to the village with mud clinging to my trousers, called everyone to the communal yard, and told them there was gold beyond the hill.

The first bowl was struck hard against the tray.

The sound was dry and sharp. After it, people began to speak.

They said I was lying. They said the bright metal was poisonous. It would destroy the old trades, make the young lazy, the greedy greedier, and the poor poorer. The village teacher said that anything of real value had no need of advertising. The potter held up his cracked hands and asked whether gold could be shaped into a bowl for rice. The whole yard laughed.

I did not.

For days I carried stones home, broke them open before the villagers, held them to the fire, and weighed them on a small scale. I argued myself hoarse that the value of gold did not depend on whether it could be eaten. No one ate a millstone, a road, or the key that opened the granary. Yet the more I spoke, the more I resembled a travelling medicine seller trying to cure a disease no one admitted having.

Then the story passed beyond me.

Refusing gold became a virtue. Any household wishing to be thought decent hung a sign at its door: **THIS HOUSE IS GOLD-FREE.** The young men formed a society for the defence of honest labour. They swore never to set foot on the western slope. Children passing my house pinched their noses, as though a dead rat lay somewhere in the yard.

I was hurt. No, that is too mild, and altogether too beautiful a word for me. I was humiliated.

I did not merely want them to believe in the mine. I wanted them to know that I had found it. I had imagined their faces when they understood, imagined the potter ceasing to laugh and the teacher addressing me in a different voice. I said I wished to share my discovery, but in the private chamber I would not confess to anyone, I also wished to be thanked.

Perhaps they smelled my pride before they saw the gold.

Perhaps not.

People often credit their wisdom for what their prejudice has merely guessed by accident. In any case, they refused me. And one night, sitting alone beside a heap of broken stone, I understood: gold did not require a vote in order to become gold.

The next morning, I apologized to the village.

I told them they were right. Gold was dangerous. Gold corrupted character. Gold could not replace the hands. I even helped pay for more **THIS HOUSE IS GOLD-FREE** signs for families that did not yet have one.

They forgave me very quickly.

A community is never more generous with forgiveness than when the offender agrees to return to his proper place beneath it.

At night, I crossed the hill.

At first, I went alone. Then came two men from town, then a cart, a small crusher, and a shed. I no longer brought gold into the village. I exchanged it where no one asked whether it made a person noble. They asked only about purity, the cost of extraction, and what it could be converted into.

It became a pump.

That year, drought emptied the village wells. I did not sell the villagers a grain of gold. I sold them water.

It became a granary.

The following rainy season, the roof of the communal storehouse collapsed. I did not ask anyone to touch gold. I merely rented them a dry place.

It became millstones, trucks, fertilizer, roadside land, and sheets of paper bearing red seals. The villagers kept their oath. They did not use gold. They only paid for the things into which gold had been converted.

Then the potter's son fell ill with fever.

I took the boy to the new clinic in the district town. In the white room, the smell of disinfectant was so clean that the human body suddenly seemed an error requiring a boundary. The potter sat outside the door with his cracked hands pressed together. He thanked me. He signed a loan paper without reading it to the end.

![A hand signing a debt note](/assets/images/nhung-nguoi-ghet-vang/plate-02-debt.png)

I could have torn up the paper.

I did not.

That is where my story forfeits the right to call itself the tale of a far-sighted man rejected by an ignorant crowd. I kept the note. I even felt a thin, cold pleasure pass through me as I looked at his signature. The bowl struck against the tray all those years ago still rang inside me; only now the sound came from beneath the feet of a man bowing his head.

I told myself it was business.

I told myself I had forced no one.

I told myself I had not given the child his fever, summoned the drought, or brought down the granary roof. I simply possessed what people needed at the moment they needed it. If the price caused pain, that was because need itself had teeth.

Every argument was perfectly clean.

Clean as the smell of disinfectant.

A few years later, the whole village worked in workshops I had built. No one was chained. Iron chains are necessary only when the owner does not yet possess the well, the granary, the road, and the date on which the debt comes due. I did not buy people. I bought the empty spaces around them, then charged them whenever they needed to cross.

They continued to hold meetings of the Gold-Free Society.

The meetings took place in a building lit by my generator. Their banners arrived on my truck. Afterwards, they drank water from my pump and agreed among themselves that decent people must resist dependence on gold.

I no longer found the contradiction amusing.

It was convenient.

Sometimes a young man asked why I had stopped trying to persuade the village. I would look at him and remember myself with mud on my trousers, holding a piece of rock in both hands as though I had lifted my heart from my chest. I had once believed that truth had a right to be known, and that anyone who found something useful had a duty to make others understand.

I no longer believed it.

If they understood the value of the mine, I would have to share the right to work it. If they learned how to turn gold into machinery, they would have no need to rent my machines. If they saw the cord running from ore to well, well to debt, debt to the hours spent in my workshops, they would no longer call their dependence a private choice.

Their ignorance did not reduce the value of gold.

It gave me the right to set the price.

Many years later, I saw another vein of gold.

![The new gold](/assets/images/nhung-nguoi-ghet-vang/plate-03-new-gold.png)

It was not in stone.

It lay behind a white input box, in lines of language appearing almost as soon as the question had been set down. People called it A.I. I used a less mystical name: machine intelligence. Yet in that first moment, I recognized precisely the feeling I had known years before - the feeling of seeing something raw, still mixed with earth, rich in poisons, yet capable of being converted into countless other things.

A.I. was not gold because it shone.

It was gold because it could be transformed.

It could become an hour of learning compressed. A first draft arriving while someone else was still afraid of the blank page. An archive searched in minutes. A person who could not program beginning to build a tool. A small group doing work that once required an entire department. An individual testing ten approaches in the time another person spent defending the dignity of the first.

Not every transformation succeeded. Ore contained rubble; A.I. contained false sentences, hollow sentences, counterfeit knowledge, and prose as glossy as waxed fruit. Anyone who did not know how to test with fire would embrace the soil and believe himself rich. Anyone who surrendered all judgment to the machine did not possess a tool. He had merely changed masters.

But gold mixed with stone did not thereby become mud.

A dangerous instrument did not become worthless merely because its user might grow lazy. Books had nourished scholars and rote learners alike. The camera had enlarged the eye and filled the world with worthless pictures. The internet had given libraries to the poor and a loudspeaker to every fool. The value of a capacity could not be judged by the worst person ever to touch it.

Yet around A.I., I heard once more the bowl struck against the tray.

It lies.

It is poisonous.

It kills creativity.

A decent person must do everything unaided.

Anyone with real ability has no need of it.

And the **THIS HOUSE IS GOLD-FREE** signs rose again. This time, they were written online, corrected by software, distributed through recommendation systems, and read on devices produced by thousands of layers of technology of which the writer could not have made a single one by hand.

I no longer ran to explain.

Why should I beg someone to accept an advantage whose refusal was leaving more of it to me?

I did not sell them A.I. I used it to learn faster, attempt more, write, translate, search, build, remove repetitive labour, and extend the work for which only a human being could take responsibility. I converted it into products, speed, free time, bargaining power, and distance. Then I sold them the things it had helped bring into being.

They might never open an A.I. tool.

But their applications might be read by a company that did. Their work might be priced against the work of someone who knew how to use it. What they watched might be selected by a system trained on their behaviour. Their manager, their competitor, the person selling to them, and the person deciding who would be seen might all have crossed the hill.

They refused to use gold.

They could not refuse the price of water set by the owner of the pump.

That is why I am pleased whenever I hear that more people are boycotting A.I.

I know there is nothing noble in the pleasure. It has the smell of the clinic where I kept the debt note instead of tearing it up. There is greed in it, retaliation, and the mean delight of watching someone voluntarily abandon the field. I will not dress it as a love of progress.

I only want to give it its proper name.

Every person who leaves the mine makes the remaining ground wider.

Every vow never to learn A.I. increases the advantage of those willing to learn.

Every community that turns ignorance into a virtue hands another community the power to define the future.

Refusing to use A.I. is a private choice. Believing that the choice places one outside the world A.I. is reorganizing is no longer purity. It is the confusion of not holding a pickaxe with not living on land someone else has begun to dig.

Once, I wanted to call the whole village up the hill.

Now, when I hear them standing at the foot of the mountain saying there is nothing above but poisoned stone, I simply nod.

Yes. Quite right.

Do not come up.

At night, the signs boycotting A.I. continue to glow on their screens. Their light falls across faces that are clean, proud, and perfectly free.

Across the hill, the new gold is still being mined.

It has no need of anyone's belief.
