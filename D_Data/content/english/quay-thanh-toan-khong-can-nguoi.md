The supermarket installed six self-checkouts where six women had once sat behind cash registers.

The change was announced on a blue sign: **FASTER. MORE CONVENIENT. MADE FOR YOU.**

Three promises, of which the last was the most suspicious.

The women disappeared over a weekend. There was no farewell. On Monday morning, customers found six new machines standing in a row, their glass so clean it reflected the confusion of anyone who approached. Each machine had a pleasant female voice. The voice had no age, no hometown, no need to sit down after eight hours on its feet, and no reason ever to swap shifts so it could collect a child.

“Please scan your first item.”

A man held a carton of milk before the reader. The machine did not see it.

“Please scan your first item.”

He turned the carton around.

“Please scan your first item.”

He looked about like a man who had failed an examination he had never enrolled in. The attendant stood a few steps away, responsible for watching all six machines at once. She came over, took the carton, found the barcode on the bottom, and passed it across the reader in one movement. The machine beeped.

“Item recognized.”

The machine possessed a quality rarely found in people: it regarded everything as having happened at the moment it noticed.

The man went on. The greens had to be weighed. The bread had to be identified from twelve nearly identical pictures. Plastic bags had to be declared. A bottle of water placed in the wrong spot made the screen flare red:

**UNEXPECTED ITEM IN BAGGING AREA.**

The man looked at the bottle. The bottle looked back through a label bearing the picture of a mountain.

The attendant returned. She touched a card to the machine, entered four digits, and converted the error into a non-error. The man thanked her. The machine thanked the man. No one thanked the bottle, although it had remained perfectly still throughout the investigation.

At the bottom of the receipt appeared a line:

**THANK YOU FOR SHOPPING WITH US.**

The word “with” performed a great deal of work. It turned a person who had found his own codes, weighed his own vegetables, packed his own bags, answered suspicion on his own behalf, and paid his own money into a partner of the store. The advantage of this partnership was that profit travelled in only one direction.

Management called the new system customer empowerment. People enjoy being empowered, especially before they have had time to ask whose work has been attached to the power.

In the first few days, many customers chose the last staffed checkout at the far end of the supermarket. They formed a long queue while the six automated machines stood empty. This was interpreted as a backward habit. Another sign appeared:

**EXPERIENCE THE FUTURE TODAY.**

The future stood ten metres away and required customers to bring their own bags.

An old woman tried the experience. She bought four oranges, a bottle of shampoo, and a packet of biscuits for diabetics. The machine asked whether she had a membership card. She said no. It asked whether she would like to register. She said no. It asked her to confirm that she did not wish to save money. She hesitated.

Declining to register was one thing. Confirming that one did not wish to save sounded like a moral defect.

She pressed Back.

The machine asked whether she had a membership card.

After three circuits, the attendant came to her rescue. The young woman spoke very slowly, although in this case it was the machine that was hard of hearing.

“Press here, Grandma.”

“Why does it keep asking me?”

“That is its process.”

Process is the name given to an event once no particular person retains enough authority to answer for it.

The old woman paid, then left her biscuits in the bagging area. The machine did not call her back. It knew the weight of every item, its price and product code, the time of the transaction, and the last four digits of her bank card. It simply did not know what an old woman walking away without her shopping looked like.

Before, a cashier might have called:

“Madam, your biscuits.”

The sentence was no faster. Nor did it appear anywhere in the digital-transformation plan. It merely spared someone a walk back through the midday heat.

A few months later, the final staffed checkout was removed. The customers had grown used to the future. Habit is the most common form of victory available to things that need not persuade us.

People learned to turn barcodes toward the reader. They learned not to put handbags on the scale. They learned to look for avocados under Vegetables rather than Imported Fruit. Older customers shopped during quiet hours so they could make mistakes with fewer witnesses. Children loved the beeps and fought to scan the groceries. At first their parents let them play; then they hurried them along because someone was waiting behind.

A job once performed for wages became a children's game, then an adult obligation.

The store announced that average checkout time had fallen by seventeen percent. It did not publish the number of times customers had looked around for help. In a report, a minute of confusion remains one minute, although it lasts much longer when someone behind you is breathing heavily.

The attendant's name was Hương. She had once been the cashier at till four. There, she had been permitted to deal with one person at a time. Now she had to watch six screens, hear six summonses, and materialize wherever a red light appeared. Her new work was called experience coordination.

Her pay had not changed.

The job title had been promoted in her place.

One evening, the network failed. All six screens displayed an empty box and froze. Customers formed lines, each holding a basket of food that belonged to them in intention but still belonged to the store in law.

Hương called the manager. The manager called technical support. Technical support told them to restart. The machines went dark, and for the first time the checkout area fell completely silent.

No female voice asked for an item to be scanned.

No prompt instructed anyone to place it in the bagging area.

No one thanked anyone.

In the silence, the six machines looked like six empty counters.

A man asked Hương how long they would have to wait. She said she did not know. He was about to lose his temper. Then he noticed the young woman before him, holding a telephone between shoulder and ear, writing down an incident code, watched by six queues as though she had personally invented malfunction.

He put down his basket.

“Take your time.”

It was the only sentence spoken that evening that did not help the system run faster.

Perhaps that was why it was the most useful.

Ten minutes later, the machines lit up again. The female voice returned intact, entirely unaware that it had been absent.

“Please scan your first item.”

The man picked up his carton of milk. This time, he knew the barcode was on the bottom.

When he had paid, the machine printed a receipt and thanked him for shopping with the store.

He folded the paper and put it in his pocket. It listed everything he had bought.

The work he had just performed was not on the list.
