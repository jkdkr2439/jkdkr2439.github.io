<p>A flower placed in the barrel of a gun is almost indecently beautiful. It offers the eye everything conscience likes to buy cheaply: innocence, courage, reconciliation, and the agreeable suspicion that violence has at last acquired taste. The picture completes a moral drama in a single frame. Steel arrives as threat; the flower enters as answer; the spectator leaves before anyone asks where the ammunition went.</p>

<p>Something simpler has happened. A gun barrel is a narrow road made for force. Put a branch inside and the road is blocked. The weapon has not been persuaded, forgiven, educated, or converted to gardening. It has merely taken in a temporary guest. Pull out the branch and the old road lies open again. The flower interrupts the gun; it does not teach the gun another life.</p>

<p>We know this kind of change well. A cruel man may lower his voice in a church without becoming kind. A locked door may seem harmless while someone stands before it with the key. A bad habit can sleep through a promise and wake the following morning refreshed. The branch lends the weapon a gentler appearance, but borrowed manners are returned more easily than borrowed money.</p>

<p>On a stage, the same meeting does something else. Lay a flower beside a rifle and the rifle begins to speak of lost youth, reluctant war, a dead lover, perhaps merely a director pleased with his own symbolism. None of this is trivial. Images gather people, loosen commands, and let us imagine an action before we dare perform it. But a sign becomes dishonest when it asks to be credited with the labour it has only announced.</p>

<p>The famous image should therefore be neither worshipped nor mocked. A branch may stop one shot by occupying the barrel. It may stop many more if the sight of it changes what people are willing to obey. Yet between the photograph and peace stand the owner of the gun, the giver of orders, the supplier of cartridges, the reward for obedience, the price of refusal, and the question of what remains after the cameras depart. Beauty can open the eyes; it cannot do all the work seen through them.</p>

<p>Civilization has always preferred a flower in a weapon to a weapon opened on a workbench. The first scene gives everyone a handsome part: the soldier may look human, the protester brave, the photographer immortal, and the audience morally present. The second scene is terribly unromantic. Someone must remove the bullets, change the order, give up an advantage, and accept blame. Peace is adored in pictures because in life it requires people to surrender something less photogenic than hatred: convenience.</p>

<p>The spectator is not innocent in this preference. We love the branch partly because it lets us enjoy the conversion of power without paying the price of converting power. The gun belongs to history, government, armies, corporations, or wicked men elsewhere; the flower belongs to us. We supply the symbol and send reality the bill. It is the most elegant division of moral labour ever devised: one person keeps the weapon, another supplies the metaphor, and both are photographed as progress.</p>

<p>Still, lasting change must begin somewhere, and sometimes it begins with an interruption. A branch in a barrel creates a pause; the pause creates a witness; the witness may notice that what seemed inevitable continued only because many hands kept it moving. If that recognition follows those hands home, enters their next command and alters their next choice, the flower ceases to be decoration. If it does not, it remains beautiful—which is not nothing, but is considerably less than peace.</p>

<p>The final question is not whether the flower defeated the gun. It is whether anything remained changed after the flower was removed. A symbol that alters the next act has entered life. A symbol that alters only the photograph has entered the archive. The gun can tolerate a flower more easily than it can tolerate a changed world; this is why power occasionally smiles for the camera.</p>

<p><strong>A flower in a gun barrel is hope. A gun that no longer requires the flower is progress.</strong></p>
