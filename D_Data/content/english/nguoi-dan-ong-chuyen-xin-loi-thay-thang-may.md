The building hired Mr Phúc because the elevators were always breaking down.

He did not know how to repair an elevator. That was not part of the job. He wore a grey suit, stood beside the steel doors, and apologized whenever they failed to open.

“We sincerely ask for the residents' understanding.”

There were two elevators in the lobby. The one on the left failed on even-numbered days. The one on the right was less methodical. Management had called technicians many times, but replacing the control unit was expensive. Hiring Mr Phúc cost less and created the impression that a human being cared about the problem.

His title was Internal Mobility Experience Coordination Specialist.

He called himself the man who stood by the elevator.

On his first morning, the right-hand car became stuck on the seventeenth floor. A woman came into the lobby with her child, pressed the button, and waited. After three minutes, she pressed it again. The number on the display remained at seventeen, like a person who had decided not to participate in the rest of the day.

Mr Phúc approached.

“We sincerely ask for your understanding. The technical department is dealing with the matter.”

“How long?”

“As soon as possible.”

“How long is that?”

During training, he had been told never to give a specific time. A specific time could be wrong. “As soon as possible” could never be wrong, because possibility was not something anyone could inspect.

“At present, we do not have an exact time.”

The woman looked at him, then at the doors. The doors had no face. So she looked back at him.

“This is the third time this month.”

“We sincerely ask for your understanding.”

The child asked:

“Did you break it?”

Mr Phúc said no.

“Then why is Mum telling you off?”

No one answered.

His work quickly proved its value. Before, residents had been angry with management, a bodiless idea usually located behind a closed office door. Now anger could stop in front of Mr Phúc. It could see a collar, sweat, and two hands folded over a stomach. Depending on its nature, it lowered or raised its voice, but at least it had found an address.

After every scolding, he wrote in his notebook:

**FEEDBACK RECEIVED.**

It was a beautiful sentence. It turned shouting into data and the person made to endure it into an input device.

Management was delighted. The number of complaints submitted by email fell by forty percent. At the quarterly meeting, the head of management pointed to the descending graph and announced a significant improvement in resident experience.

The elevators continued to fail at exactly the same rate.

Some people felt sorry for Mr Phúc. They told him it was not his fault and took the stairs. Others became angrier, because pity seemed to force them to be polite to the system through a man with no authority. Some brought him water. Some asked how much he was paid to endure such work.

He usually smiled.

“A job is a job.”

The sentence was modest enough to end their curiosity and sad enough to make them feel they understood.

In truth, there were parts of the work he liked. He remembered the residents' schedules. He knew the old woman on the eighth floor needed a chair, the doctor on the twelfth often worked nights, and the girl on the fifth was afraid of enclosed spaces. When the elevators worked, he held the doors for people running toward them. When they failed, he pointed out the emergency stairs and carried shopping bags.

None of this appeared in his job description. It was therefore neither measured nor, very often, anything less than the most useful work he did.

One afternoon, an elevator stopped between the ninth and tenth floors. Five people were inside. The alarm sounded in the lobby. Mr Phúc ran to the intercom.

“Please remain calm. The technicians are on their way.”

A man's voice asked:

“How long?”

“As soon as possible.”

For the first time, he heard the phrase from his own mouth as others heard it. Light, rounded, with nowhere to take hold.

A child began crying inside. A woman said there was no phone signal. Someone struck the door.

Mr Phúc sat on the floor with his back against the steel.

“Is there a little girl in there?”

The crying stopped for a moment.

“What's your name?”

“An.”

“What class are you in, An?”

The girl did not answer. Her mother said second grade.

Mr Phúc told An that elevators were like schoolchildren: sometimes, halfway through a lesson, they forgot what came next. An asked whether the teacher would punish it. He said yes, but the teacher was stuck in traffic. The girl laughed. The adults inside fell silent, perhaps because the story was absurd, perhaps because at least it was a sentence that had not come from an incident-response manual.

Forty minutes later, the technicians opened the doors.

No one was hurt. Management issued a notice:

**THE INCIDENT WAS RESOLVED SAFELY AND PROMPTLY.**

Forty minutes, once placed beside the word “safely,” became promptly.

An's mother sent a letter thanking Mr Phúc. The head of management printed it, framed it, and hung it beside the duty desk. Underneath, they added a slogan:

**PEOPLE ARE AT THE CENTRE OF EVERY EXPERIENCE.**

Mr Phúc stood reading it for a long time. He could not tell whether he was a person or a centre.

The following month, the left-hand elevator failed for three days in succession. Residents demanded a replacement. Management said the year's budget had already been allocated. They extended Mr Phúc's hours and issued him a new tie bearing the building's logo.

The uniform was improved in place of the equipment.

Mr Phúc continued to apologize.

An apology, used in the proper place, can begin a repair. Used for too long, it becomes soundproofing. The people on one side continue to speak; those with power on the other can no longer hear them clearly.

Then one morning, Mr Phúc did not come to work.

Nothing tragic had happened. He was simply at home with a fever. The security guard placed a sign beside the elevators:

**WE APOLOGIZE FOR THE INCONVENIENCE.**

That day, both elevators failed at once.

Residents came into the lobby, read the sign, and gathered around. There was no one to ask how long. No face to look at. No man in a grey suit to receive the first portion of their anger.

They began calling management.

After the seventeenth call, the head of management came down. He wore a white shirt, no name badge, and kept repeating that the technicians were on their way.

A resident asked:

“How long?”

The manager replied:

“As soon as possible.”

This time, no one understood.

Two weeks later, the control unit was replaced.

Mr Phúc returned when both elevators were running smoothly. Management offered to move him to reception, as his former position was no longer necessary. He sat at a desk near the entrance, gave directions to visitors, and accepted parcels.

Each morning, residents stepped into the elevator. The doors closed and the numbers rose steadily. No one looked at the doors and thought of him.

It was a sign that the equipment was working well.

One afternoon, An came home from school and saw him at the new desk.

“Don't you apologize for the elevator anymore?”

“It doesn't break down now.”

“Did it apologize to you?”

Mr Phúc smiled.

Behind the girl, the elevator opened on the right floor, at the right moment, in perfect silence.
