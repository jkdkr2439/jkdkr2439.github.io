<section id="s1"><p>The first thing I took out of other people's promises was the word always.</p><p>It was usually standing where a date should have been. I will always be there for you, a man would write, when the matter in question was whether he would collect his daughter on Saturday. I deleted it and asked what time. This was considered one of my particular skills.</p><p>We worked above a shop that sold electric rice cookers. When the shopkeeper demonstrated the largest model, steam rose past the window beside my desk and for a little while our room smelled of a lunch none of us had to prepare. There were eleven of us on the afternoon shift. The software made the first attempt. We dealt with whatever could not be settled by choosing warmer, shorter or more sincere.</p><p>A customer could ask for words to accompany money sent home, a message after an argument, something to write beneath a photograph of a dead colleague. They supplied details. We asked for more. We were not allowed to promise that the recipient would forgive them. There was a separate button for dissatisfaction with the writing, and another for dissatisfaction with the reply. People used the second one more often.</p><p>At the beginning I had thought I would be doing this until I found work that deserved to be described properly. By the third year I had learned to recognize a useful sentence. Once, a man who drove delivery vans had sent his wife a paragraph which began with an account of the traffic and ended with an account of his boss. Buried in the middle was the fact that he was afraid to come home because their son no longer ran to the door. We moved that part to the beginning. The wife called him. He came back the following week to have us write a message to his landlord.</p><p>I told this story when people smiled at my job. I did not tell them that we offered a discount for returning customers.</p><p>On the Monday of the move, a woman asked me to improve a message to her father. She had written that she would come whenever he needed her. I asked whether she could name a day.</p><p>While I waited for her answer, Liên called.</p><p>“Thursday. Four o'clock,” she said. “Have you put it in?”</p><p>I opened the calendar on my phone. My sister had sent an invitation called Carry things. I had not accepted it.</p><p>“I've got it,” I said, accepting the invitation.</p><p>“Have you asked?”</p><p>“Asked who?”</p><p>“At work. To leave.”</p><p>I moved the invitation out of the way. There was another appointment underneath it, one I could do on my phone.</p><p>“I can sort that out.”</p><p>There was a clatter at her end. She was at the shop where she altered trousers, holding the phone between her cheek and her shoulder. In the background someone was insisting that a waist could be let out further.</p><p>“Hang on,” she said, away from the phone. Then, to me: “Don't leave it till the day.”</p><p>“I'll come,” I said. I meant it, and wanted her to stop asking.</p><p>The woman in the chat had written Saturday, possibly.</p><p>I asked her to remove possibly.</p></section>

<section id="s2"><p>My mother had lived upstairs for twenty-six years. The stairwell was narrower at the top than at the bottom, or seemed to be when you were carrying anything. As a child I had taken those stairs two at a time. Later I noticed the landing halfway up. Later still, my mother began leaving a plastic chair there.</p><p>On Tuesday I found the chair occupied by a sack of clothes.</p><p>“Don't take those,” she said from above. “They're for the woman opposite.”</p><p>There were piles on the floor, but there was nothing disorderly about them. One was going with her, one was going to the neighbors, and one was waiting for someone to persuade her it still had a use. My sister was kneeling beside a carton, trying to fit a saucepan into the hollow beneath a smaller saucepan's handle.</p><p>“You can put those in another box,” I told her.</p><p>“The bottom's going.”</p><p>I looked at the saucepan.</p><p>“The box, anh. Under your foot. Don't stand on it.”</p><p>I went downstairs and asked the rice seller for one. When I came back, the pans had been made to fit.</p><p>The new room was beyond the city, near the garment workshop where Liên had been offered a position checking finished work. It had a bathroom on the same level and a window that opened into a strip of earth. My mother's cousin lived two doors away. Liên showed me a photograph. The floor looked wet, though she said it was the reflection from the tiles.</p><p>I asked whether we should look at a few more places.</p><p>She scrolled backward through her phone and showed me six.</p><p>Mother was sitting at the sewing machine. She was not sewing. The machine had a dark iron pedal shaped like a little gate, with a section polished silver where her heel rested. She had put her feet beside it, not on it. On the table were a roll of tape, a tin of buttons and an old receipt she was using to calculate something.</p><p>“It'll be cooler,” she said. “Downstairs.”</p><p>For years I had imagined buying her a house. I knew the shape of its windows and the tree I would plant outside. Faced with a photograph of a room I could help pay for, I had asked about damp.</p><p>“And this?” I touched the machine.</p><p>“The man downstairs knows someone,” Liên said.</p><p>“We're taking it.”</p><p>Liên looked up from the carton.</p><p>“Have you found someone?”</p><p>“To do what?”</p><p>“Take it down. I asked the driver. He brings the vehicle, that's all.”</p><p>“I'll be here.”</p><p>“It needs two.”</p><p>I tried lifting one end of the table. It moved, but I had caught the drawer with my knee. Mother reached over and held the machine steady.</p><p>Mother put her hand over the receipt.</p><p>“Look first. There's not much room.”</p><p>“We'll make room. Don't agree to sell it yet.”</p><p>I said it pleasantly, as though space were something that might be produced by keeping everyone calm. Liên folded the flaps of the carton. One would not close. She pulled the tape between her teeth, stopped, and looked round for the scissors.</p><p>I remembered that sound from evenings when we were small: tape coming off the roll, the pedal slowing, my mother asking one of us to stand straight. She used the machine table to mark the hems of our school trousers. We had to keep our knees still. I remembered being impatient with her fingers at my ankles, and wondered why this particular impatience had survived so clearly when I could not remember most of the things I had been in a hurry to do.</p><p>“Thursday, then,” I said.</p><p>“Come in shoes,” Liên said. “Not those sandals.”</p><p>I paid for the noodles we ate that evening. Mother picked the pieces of spring onion out of hers and placed them on the lid. When I offered to do it, she covered the bowl with her hand.</p><p>“Eat yours. It'll go soft.”</p></section>

<section id="s3"><p>The client I remember best from that week was a mechanic called Vinh. He sent a voice message because, he said, he did not write well. He had told his daughter, during an argument about the man she lived with, not to come back. Now he wanted to apologize without making it sound as though he approved of everything.</p><p>The software offered a passage about respecting her journey. He asked what journey.</p><p>I called him through the service. He was in his workshop. Between answers he shouted instructions to someone about a wheel.</p><p>“What exactly do you want her to know?”</p><p>“That the house is still her house.”</p><p>I typed a sentence inviting them both to dinner.</p><p>“Not him,” he said when I read it back. “I said her.”</p><p>I deleted the last few words.</p><p>“Does she know you mean only her?”</p><p>“She knows me.”</p><p>Someone at his end started a motor. He shouted to have it turned off. When it stopped, he was still speaking, and I missed the beginning of his answer.</p><p>“Sorry. What was that?”</p><p>“She won't read it. That's what I'm telling you. I went round and she sent the security man out.”</p><p>“To her home?”</p><p>“Work. I don't know where she lives now.”</p><p>The message I had been preparing assumed they were ready to discuss a visit. I saved it without sending it.</p><p>“Could we start with what you said that day? About not coming back?”</p><p>“You can make it less harsh.”</p><p>“I mean you could say you're sorry you said it.”</p><p>He asked whether that would mean the other man could come too. I said it did not have to settle that question. We spent some time on this. Twice he returned to something the man had done, and twice I thought he was withdrawing the apology. The second time he said he was telling me, not asking me to put it in the message.</p><p>In the end we wrote something shorter. He was sorry he had told her to go. He would not turn up at her workplace again. She did not have to answer. He asked me to add that he had found the spare key to her motorbike.</p><p>“Will you leave it somewhere for her?”</p><p>“She can come and get it.”</p><p>I asked whether he meant the house. He said of course. I read the message back, including the sentence about not having to answer. For a moment he said nothing.</p><p>“Her aunt, then. I'll leave it there. She sees her.”</p><p>We put that in.</p><p>He read the message aloud, stumbling at a word I had chosen. I changed it. He read it again. It sounded better the second time, less like me.</p><p>Hảo was listening from the next desk. She supervised our shift. Before this she had spent four years answering complaints for an internet provider. She could remain courteous while being called a thief, and she knew when courtesy was making the other person angrier.</p><p>“That's one for the demonstration,” she said after the call.</p><p>“We'd need permission.”</p><p>“I mean the kind of thing. Not his story.”</p><p>The service was launching a new package on Friday. A customer would be able to leave an exchange open, bring back a reply, and pay for help with what came next. Several of us disliked the name the sales team had given it. Hảo disliked the fact that nobody had arranged who would cover the additional shifts. Both objections were entered in the meeting notes. Only hers was assigned to a person.</p><p>On Wednesday she asked me to stay late the following afternoon. The demonstration messages had been written by people who thought every apology needed a childhood memory. One example involved a coffee cup a woman had supposedly kept through three moves. Another involved a dead dog. It was the same dog in a condolence message two slides later.</p><p>“You can stop us embarrassing ourselves,” Hảo said.</p><p>I told her I had to help my mother move.</p><p>“Four, isn't it?”</p><p>“Yes.”</p><p>“If I get you the examples before lunch, can you finish those first?”</p><p>That sounded possible. I said so. She went back to her desk. A little later she asked whether I could also check changes after the rehearsal.</p><p>“From home?” I asked.</p><p>“We may need you in the room. I don't know yet.”</p><p>She did not say that the renewal of our contracts depended on Friday. She had already told us this at the meeting, in the same voice with which she had asked us not to leave open food beside the keyboards. We had two months left on the current arrangement. I had a deposit to contribute to, and Liên's first full wage at the new place would not arrive for weeks.</p><p>“How late?” I asked.</p><p>“I'll try to keep it short. If you have to go, tell me before you go. Don't just disappear.”</p><p>I said I would. On my calendar, Thursday still said Carry things. I had not told her I could stay, but I had stopped telling her I needed to leave.</p></section>

<section id="s4"><p>At three on Thursday, my sister sent a photograph of the cartons beside the stairs. The chair was no longer on the landing. She had tied it to another chair with a strip of blue cloth.</p><p>At half past three, I was correcting a demonstration in which a woman assured her brother that she had always supported his choices. According to the background notes, she had refused to attend his wedding. I put a comment in the margin. We cannot keep this sentence.</p><p>Then I opened the conversation with my mother.</p><p>I wrote that I was still at work. It looked careless. I added that something important had come up. This made the move sound unimportant. I deleted it and started again.</p><p>Hảo set a paper cup beside me. She had bought coffee for everyone. Hers was still full.</p><p>In the end I pasted my draft into the same box our clients used. I selected a warmer tone. The suggestion came back quickly. It explained that I was working hard to give her a more comfortable life, and that I hoped she would understand this small sacrifice now would make things easier later.</p><p>I took out sacrifice. I could hear the ugliness of it. I left the more comfortable life.</p><p>I added that I would come on Saturday and take care of whatever remained. Then I sent it.</p><p>Mother replied with one word.</p><p>Yes.</p><p>I knew her version of yes. It could mean she had heard, or that the rice was cooked, or that she wanted a caller to finish speaking so she could turn the gas down. She said it to shopkeepers whose prices she had no intention of accepting. Yet I looked at this one and found nothing uncertain in it.</p><p>I transferred more money than I had planned. The amount was large enough to make me sit back for a moment after confirming it. I sent the receipt to Liên.</p><p>She wrote: Call the man about the machine yourself, then.</p><p>I asked for his number.</p><p>While she was sending it, Hảo brought over a change to the presentation. The new package was no longer going to be called an assistant. It would be described as someone who stayed with you through the difficult conversation. We had to remove a sentence explaining that different editors might respond at different times, because the sentence interrupted the flow. Hảo said we could put the information in the details below. I said that was fine. We were both tired.</p><p>When I returned to my phone, there were three messages from Liên. The first contained the number. The second said the vehicle was waiting. The third said Never mind.</p><p>I called her. She did not answer. I called Mother. There was the hollow bumping sound of a phone inside a bag, and then her voice, far away.</p><p>“We're going now.”</p><p>“Did they take everything?”</p><p>“It's raining a bit. Wait, let me get in.”</p><p>I heard Liên tell her where to put her foot. Mother said she would call later. I told her not to worry about anything.</p><p>At nine we ate bread at our desks. At eleven Hảo got permission for the overtime payment. She came down the row telling each of us separately, as if good news could be made larger that way.</p><p>On the journey home I thought of calling again. The time on my phone persuaded me that it would be kinder not to.</p></section>

<section id="s5"><p>The launch went well enough. There were questions about price, and one about whether the service might encourage people to avoid difficult conversations. Hảo answered that some people were already avoiding them, and that a beginning could matter. I thought of Vinh. I was glad she had said it.</p><p>On Saturday I went to the old room to collect what remained and return the key. Mother had asked me to look behind the door for a cloth bag. She thought she might have forgotten it.</p><p>The landlord let me in. Without the furniture, the room was not as big as I had expected. There were pale squares on the wall and a patch of floor near the window where the tiles looked new. I stood on it before I understood that this was where the machine had been.</p><p>I found a button in the groove beside the skirting. It was white, ordinary, with four holes. I put it in my pocket. There was no bag behind the door.</p><p>Downstairs I asked about the machine. The man who had bought it had already sent it to his brother in another province. It worked well, the landlord said. My mother had shown him how to wind the bobbin. They had lowered the price because the drawers stuck.</p><p>“She didn't mind?” I asked.</p><p>He looked at me.</p><p>“She named the price.”</p><p>I had been prepared to discover she had been cheated. It would have given me something to do with the afternoon. I could have phoned, argued, paid. Instead the landlord told me she had emptied the drawers herself, tipped the loose needles into a small tin, and tested the pedal for the buyer.</p><p>There was a boy on the landing where the chair used to stand. He was playing a game with the sound turned up. Beneath him was Mother's little sewing stool. She had kept folded cloth on it to make it higher. Now the cloth was gone and the boy's feet hung clear of the ground.</p><p>“She said I could have it,” he said when he saw me looking.</p><p>“I know.”</p><p>I did not know, but I had no other answer that would not frighten him.</p><p>On the street I took out my phone and looked for photographs of the machine. I found my mother's birthday, two Tet holidays, Liên holding a cake in a box because there was nowhere to set it down. In most of the photographs the machine was partly hidden by someone's body. I enlarged a corner and lost the grain of the wood.</p><p>For a while I considered asking the landlord for the buyer's number. Then I remembered that I already had a number I had not called.</p></section>

<section id="s6"><p>The new room had a patch of earth outside, just as Liên's photograph had shown. Someone had planted spring onions in a cut-down cooking-oil container. The window opened, but only as far as a drainpipe would allow.</p><p>Mother was asleep when I arrived. Her feet were uncovered. Liên was arranging clothes on a metal rack. She had hung her work shirts at one end and Mother's blouses at the other, with a space between them for things not yet unpacked.</p><p>“I couldn't find the bag,” I whispered.</p><p>“It's here. She put it in the rice pot.”</p><p>I put down the fruit I had brought. There was a second bag of the same fruit on the floor.</p><p>“You should have waited with the machine.”</p><p>Liên took a shirt off its hanger and buttoned it before hanging it up again.</p><p>“He wouldn't give any more for it.”</p><p>“What?”</p><p>“The drawers. You have to pull them up to open them. He showed me.”</p><p>“I'm not talking about the price. I said I'd take care of it.”</p><p>She put the hanger on the bed. She had left one button undone.</p><p>“I sent you his number.”</p><p>“I was in the middle of something. You knew that.”</p><p>“Yes. I told him. Then he asked me again.”</p><p>I began explaining about the launch. Partway through, she reached past me for an empty bag.</p><p>“Are you listening?”</p><p>“I'm listening. These have to go back to dì.”</p><p>She was stacking bowls with a pattern I did not recognize. I moved the bag of fruit so she could put them down.</p><p>“The extra money's come through,” I said. “If that wasn't enough, you could have told me.”</p><p>She looked at the sleeping shape on the bed and lowered her voice.</p><p>“It was enough. There's some left. I'll send you the receipt.”</p><p>“I don't want a receipt.”</p><p>“Then what do you want me to tell you?”</p><p>I almost said that I had been doing my best. It was not an agreement. She picked up the hanger and tried to put it on the rack with the shirt facing the wrong way. For a moment neither of us spoke.</p><p>“The driver wanted to go,” she said. “Mẹ was still upstairs. I couldn't find her bag. I thought you'd called him by then.”</p><p>“The driver?”</p><p>“No. The man about the machine.”</p><p>I had not called either of them. On Thursday I had still thought there would be a point when they would stop and ask me for a decision.</p><p>“You could have rung me again.”</p><p>She left the shirt on the bed.</p><p>Mother moved in her sleep. We both waited. From outside came a woman's voice asking for the borrowed bowls. Liên took the bag to the door. I held it open while she went through. The fruit stayed where I had put it.</p><p>Later Mother woke and asked whether I had eaten. She wanted to know how the presentation had gone. I told her it was fine. She asked whether I had worn the blue shirt. She had shortened its sleeves two years before.</p><p>I said I was sorry about the machine.</p><p>“It didn't hit anything on the way down,” she said. “I was afraid of that turn.”</p><p>“I mean I could get you another. Not such a heavy one.”</p><p>She rubbed the base of her thumb with the fingers of her other hand.</p><p>“Your blue shirt still fits, doesn't it?”</p><p>“It isn't for my clothes, mẹ. For you. If you felt like doing a little.”</p><p>She looked toward the doorway, where Liên had come back and was rinsing a cloth in a bucket.</p><p>“Don't buy anything yet. People see a machine, they bring things.”</p><p>I began to say she could refuse. Liên wrung out the cloth and asked Mother where she wanted the small tin. Mother pointed to the bed, then changed her mind. There was a place on the wall where she wanted a shelf, low enough to reach without standing. She asked me to hold the tin there so she could see.</p><p>With my arm out, I could see how little space there was between the bed and the bathroom door. The old table would have narrowed it further.</p><p>I wanted to ask whether she was happy. Liên talked about the new workshop. There would be a probation period, and she was worried about remembering the names of the different finishing faults. She had a notebook with drawings of seams. I had never seen her draw before. One sketch was so small that she brought it close to the light to show Mother.</p><p>For a few minutes the two of them were interested in something I could not help with. I sat and ate. Outside, water from an air conditioner fell into an empty paint tin, irregularly, in pairs and then not at all.</p></section>

<section id="s7"><p>That night, back in my own room, I received a reply from Vinh. His daughter had said he could leave the key with her aunt. He wanted to thank her, but he was afraid that thanking her would make it a bigger thing than she intended.</p><p>I wrote that no answer was necessary. Then I added that if he did answer, he could simply say when he would leave the key.</p><p>He sent me a photograph the next morning. A key on a flowered plate. Nothing beneath it. I had no way of knowing whether his daughter ever came for it.</p><p>I began a message to Mother. The first version said I understood how hard this had been for her. I read it twice. I had spent less than three hours in the new room, much of it feeling injured by my sister. I did not understand how hard it had been.</p><p>I asked the software for another way to begin. It suggested acknowledging that I had let her down. This was accurate. It also sounded like the first sentence of a message after which she would be required to assure me I had not.</p><p>I left the box open.</p><p>There was a message from Liên. She was going to have to leave at half past five on Monday for the new job. The cousin could come over in the morning, but had to take her husband to an appointment after lunch. Would I stay until Liên got home?</p><p>I looked at Monday. There was a review meeting at eleven and my shift began at one. I could work from the new room if Hảo agreed. The connection might be poor. I would need my headset. If I stayed Sunday night, I would not have to make the journey before dawn.</p><p>I wrote to Hảo with the hours I needed. I packed the headset, the charger and a shirt.</p></section>

<section id="s8"><p>On Sunday night Liên cleared a corner of the table for my computer. The table wobbled when I typed. We tried a folded bus ticket under one leg, then a piece of carton. I asked for the scissors.</p><p>“Don't take it apart,” she said. “It's dì's.”</p><p>“I'm cutting the carton.”</p><p>She looked at the piece in my hand, then gave me the scissors. “They're in that drawer, if you need them again.”</p><p>Mother asked what I was cutting.</p><p>“A piece for the table.”</p><p>“Fold it twice. It'll squash.”</p><p>I folded it twice.</p><p>Liên showed me where she had put the bowls, which switch worked the bathroom light, and how far the kettle could be filled without spilling when Mother lifted it. None of this took long to explain. I wrote some of it down. When I asked which cup Mother used, they both pointed to the same one, a glass with a crack in the handle that was not to be used for hot water.</p><p>“Then why keep it?” I asked.</p><p>“For cold water,” Mother said.</p><p>Liên laughed, unexpectedly, and I laughed too. Mother looked from one of us to the other, not quite pleased to be the cause, and told us to put the cups back.</p><p>After they lay down, I sat at the table and checked the connection. It did not stand out.</p><p>Liên's new shoes were by the door. The soles were clean. She had stuffed the toes with newspaper to keep them from creasing. I do.”</p><p>“I'll get up with you.”</p><p>“Are you going somewhere?”</p><p>“No.”</p><p>She was quiet. I heard her check the latch on her bag.</p><p>“You can go back to sleep after I leave.”</p><p>I put the phone beside the glass. The alarm screen was still open. There was no reason to make a sentence of it.</p><p>At five, when it rang, I reached for it before I remembered where I was. My sister was sitting on the edge of the bed, bending to put on her shoes.
