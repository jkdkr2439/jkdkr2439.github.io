<p>“God, I need to shit. I should go.” This is the ordinary person. The body issues a notice, consciousness reads it, and the legs escort civilisation to the bathroom. No existential committee is summoned. The stool has not yet become a symbol, an argument or a crisis of Western reason. It merely wishes to leave.</p>

<p>The writer is less fortunate. A cramp arrives and immediately acquires weather. “The pain curled inside me like a small animal ashamed of its own existence.” He sits upon the toilet as an exiled prince recovering a porcelain throne. The smell carries childhood, humiliation and a lost summer. His business is feeling: he takes a bodily event and gives it atmosphere, memory and a pulse another person can borrow.</p>

<p>The philosopher is more ambitious. He cannot permit waste to remain merely waste. “If the body defines itself by what it rejects, is identity founded upon exclusion? Is excrement matter without value, or value that has completed its historical task? Does truth enter the world through the mouth and depart through the anus, altered only by consciousness?” He has not yet relieved himself, but Being has already developed abdominal complications.</p>

<p>The writer gives the stool a mood; the philosopher gives it a position in the universe. One turns discomfort into human feeling, the other drafts the bowel into the search for truth. Both may produce something beautiful. Beauty, however, is an exceptionally well-dressed witness and a notoriously unreliable mechanic.</p>

<p>The epistemologist is indecent in a different way. He asks how long the digestive journey takes, which variables alter it, whether meals, routine, stress and bodily signals can help predict the next visit, what observations are trustworthy, and what evidence would distinguish a useful pattern from an attractive superstition. He does not begin by asking what the stool means. He asks how we could know anything responsible about it at all.</p>

<p>These are four legitimate orientations until one begins impersonating another. The ordinary person acts. The writer makes experience shareable. The philosopher tests the reach of concepts. The epistemologist exposes the mechanism of knowing, including its errors. Confusion begins when emotion presents itself as explanation, when a grand question presents itself as an answer, or when decorative obscurity demands the social privileges of discovery.</p>

<p>This is where AI enters, not as a silicon muse but as a library condemned to work overtime. Many people do not ask it to “write beautifully.” They use it to enlarge a question: expose variables, compare hypotheses, cross domains, locate missing evidence, test counterexamples and return to see which mechanism survives. They are not asking a machine to lend them a soul. They are asking it to stop the soul from mistaking three books and a rainy afternoon for the whole of reality.</p>

<p>Some writers and professional custodians of depth find this intolerable. They announce that AI has no childhood, no wounds, no love, no death and therefore no right to language. The speech is so noble that one nearly forgets to ask what all their authentic suffering has actually explained. Experience, like inherited silver, is often displayed most proudly by those who have never learned what it is for.</p>

<p>Their anger is not always a defence of art or truth. Sometimes it is the alarm of a profession discovering that what it called depth can be manufactured from solemn nouns, borrowed anguish and the names of several distinguished corpses. AI is cruel to decorative profundity because it reproduces the decoration before the coffee cools. When a machine can deliver alienation, the abyss, humanity and the crisis of the subject on command, the author faces an impolite possibility: perhaps the depth was located in the upholstery.</p>

<p>The philosopher who merely renames constipation “the resistance of Being” is no safer. A difficult vocabulary can enlarge thought, but it can also provide first-class accommodation for an empty room. There are sentences so profound that no observation can reach them, no contradiction can injure them and no reader can determine whether they are wrong. Their immortality is assured by the simple precaution of never becoming alive.</p>

<p>Of course AI produces rubbish. This is a curious accusation from a species that has produced rubbish by hand for thousands of years and called the method tradition. A machine can be lazy, derivative and falsely confident; so can a graduate with excellent punctuation. The relevant distinction is not between tool and purity, but between inquiry and ornament, between a claim that risks correction and a performance that survives by refusing to become clear.</p>

<p>Place the same stool before the four people. The ordinary person asks where the toilet is. The writer asks how the moment feels. The philosopher asks what rejection reveals about being. The epistemologist asks which process produced the observation, what was lost in measuring it, and how the account could be tested. AI is most threatening not when it imitates the second or third voice, but when it helps millions of ordinary people practise the fourth.</p>

<p>The serious writer and the serious philosopher need not fear this. They can understand mechanism without surrendering beauty, and pursue truth without treating fog as evidence. The finest prose does not compete with explanation; it makes explanation memorable. The finest philosophy does not resent instruments; it clarifies what an instrument can and cannot establish.</p>

<p>So I do not ask why writers have a problem with AI. I ask why some people, after spending their careers praising the complexity of man, become furious when man acquires another instrument for complex thought. Perhaps they do not fear that machines will write like people. Perhaps they fear that ordinary people, assisted by machines, will begin asking precise questions that educated people once enjoyed answering vaguely.</p>

<p>When a person is looking for a mechanism, do not condemn him for using AI merely because you have used your soul, your suffering and the entire history of Being, yet still failed to digest the question.</p>
