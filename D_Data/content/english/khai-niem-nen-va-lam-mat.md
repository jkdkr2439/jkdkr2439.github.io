On a kitchen table lie a porcelain cup, a paper cup, a cracked mug, and a
child's drawing of a cup. A person can use one word across all four. That small
achievement is easy to overlook. The objects differ in material, shape,
durability, purpose, and relation to use. Thought travels because it does not
carry every difference at once.

A concept is a device of selective sameness.

> Concepts make thought possible by preserving differences relevant to a
> purpose and suppressing others. Their power and their danger arise from the
> same compression.

## Without compression there is no thought

If every encounter remained incomparable with every other, learning would not
generalize. We could not recognize tools, symptoms, promises, games, or people.
We could not ask whether a new case resembles an old one in the respect that
matters.

Conceptual compression is therefore not a regrettable corruption of pure
particularity. It is a condition of reasoning. The trouble begins when a useful
compression forgets its purpose and presents its boundaries as inevitable.

## Membership is not always one thing

Some concepts are governed by explicit rules. Others cluster around typical
cases. Still others depend on use, history, institution, or contested value.
The word *triangle* behaves differently from *disease*, *art*, *violence*, or
*expert*.

Demanding one theory of membership for every concept creates false clarity.
For each important term, ask what makes a case count here: necessary
conditions, resemblance to prototypes, causal history, institutional
decision, practical consequence, or some combination.

## Boundary cases reveal the machinery

Clear cases allow a classification to operate invisibly. Borderline cases make
its rule visible. A novel condition at the edge of a diagnostic category can
show which symptoms were weighted and why. A dispute about whether a machine
output counts as authorship can expose competing concepts of intention,
control, labor, and responsibility.

The boundary is not automatically arbitrary because it is disputed. Nor is it
automatically natural because an institution has fixed it. Boundaries can be
better or worse relative to explanatory and practical purposes.

## Compression hides distributions

A group label can support a true average while concealing enormous internal
variation. “The public,” “experts,” “young people,” and “the market” often
appear as single actors even when the underlying population contains opposed
interests and unequal influence.

Whenever a collective noun performs causal work, reopen it. Who acted? How
many? Under what selection rule? Which members carried the effect? Which were
merely counted inside the name?

## Concepts can become traps

A classification begins as a tool. It becomes a trap when:

- cases are forced to serve the category rather than test it;
- internal variation is treated as noise by definition;
- the label carries praise or blame that the evidence did not establish;
- changing purposes are hidden beneath stable vocabulary;
- disagreement over boundaries is misreported as disagreement over facts.

The remedy is not to abandon concepts. It is to keep a decompression route.
We should be able to return from the name to representative cases, dimensions,
exceptions, and the purpose for which the grouping was made.

## Classification changes the classified

Some categories merely sort objects. Others enter the lives of the people they
classify. A diagnosis can bring relief, stigma, treatment, community, or a new
vocabulary of self-description. An administrative category can determine
which door opens and which form is refused. People respond to the
classification, and their response changes the population the category was
supposed merely to describe.

This feedback does not make the category unreal. Money, citizenship, and rank
depend on institutions and still produce consequences sturdy enough to break a
life. The useful contrast is not real versus invented. It is which parts of a
classification answer to recurring structure, which depend on collective
rules, and how the rule changes the cases passing through it.

## The moral laundering of nouns

A noun can complete an argument before the evidence arrives. Call a person a
victim and injury enters the room; call the same person a complainant and the
claim remains procedurally open; call that person an opportunist and motive has
already been tried.

Such words may prove accurate. Their danger lies in arriving early, then
pretending they merely took attendance. A classification with moral content
should display the observations and inferences that licensed it. Otherwise
grammar performs the conviction and reasoning arrives later to collect the
body.

## A concept audit

Ask:

1. Which differences does this concept preserve?
2. Which differences does it suppress?
3. What purpose makes that trade useful?
4. What determines membership?
5. Which boundary cases place pressure on the rule?
6. Does the label import an evaluation?
7. Can the category be decompressed when the decision becomes costly?

Concepts make experience portable. Language makes concepts public, repeatable,
and governable. Once a distinction receives a name, however, it can begin to
look as though the world arrived already divided by that word.
