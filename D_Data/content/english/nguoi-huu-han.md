*By: Kevin T.N a.k.a Lucis The Lord*

The first woman who came to me did not want the truth.

Her daughter had been dead for thirteen days. She had stopped weeping in front
of others, but her hands continued a strange labor: closing, opening, closing
again, as though they still held something small that she could not bear to
see. She sat before me and asked whether I knew how to return the dead.

I said no.

She asked whether I knew how to let the living follow them.

Again, I said no.

She looked at me with the exhaustion of someone who had passed through many
doors only to meet another wall. I understood that if I spoke to her then about
truth, truth would be merely one more refusal to give her somewhere to sit.

I asked her to place a hand upon her belly. Nothing grand. Notice the breath
entering. Notice it leaving. When the child returned in memory, do not drive
her away. When the wish to die appeared, do not receive it as a command. Give
it its proper size: a wish passing through a body that had not slept, had not
eaten, had not been held for thirteen days.

She tried.

After a long while, her hands stopped closing. She slept on the floor. Sleep
did not return her daughter, prove the world just, or solve the secrecy of
death. It kept her alive for one night.

That was when I first understood that a tool need not be true of the whole
world to save one person.

The next morning she called what I had shown her the path.

I told her it was only a handhold.

She did not hear me. A drowning person cannot distinguish a raft from a shore.
At the instant something raises his mouth above the water, that floating thing
seems to possess the law of the entire sea.

She returned and brought others. A man who had lost his husband. A farmer who
had lost his field. A woman carrying a pain for which no one else had a name.
A merchant frightened of tomorrow. A wealthy official who discovered each
night an empty room inside his chest. They sat. They breathed. They watched a
sensation arrive, change, and leave. One slept. One stopped striking his son.
One put down a knife. One discovered that fear was not a god speaking in his
head, only a body preparing for a disaster that had not happened.

I do not despise such results. One more night of life is still a night. A hand
putting down a knife does not need permission from philosophy.

But soon they began to say that whatever lessened their pain must be the
highest truth. They used medicine for a fever to measure every temperature of
existence. They looked upon a lover and saw attachment. They looked upon a
carpenter building a house and saw attachment. They looked upon a dispossessed
man's anger and saw attachment again. Every movement toward the world received
the name of a chain.

From a method of not drowning, they made a doctrine about the wickedness of
water.

I wanted to stop them, but I had given them a word too wide: suffering.

I had watched sensation call desire, desire call action, action leave a mark in
memory, and memory prepare the next sensation before the world arrived. An
insult did not end when its sound faded. It returned at night and borrowed the
enemy's voice from inside us. A moment of praise did not end in pleasure. It
created hunger for the next praise. We pursued what pleased us, resisted what
hurt us, and were dragged by pursuit and resistance alike.

I called this being-dragged suffering.

The word was useful. It gathered wounds that appeared unrelated into one
mechanism. Yet every clear name conceals an appetite. It wants to possess not
only what it illuminates, but everything surrounding the light.

The second person who came to me did not need to survive the night. He wanted
to dismantle the trap.

He recorded each link. A sound touched the ear. The body answered before the
sound completed its meaning. Pleasure or displeasure arose. From it came the
urge to keep, reject, repeat, or flee. An urge repeated became a habit. Habit
learned to speak in the first person. At last a bundle of responses stood up,
called itself I, and began defending the history that had made it.

He asked where attachment lived.

I told him it did not live in the thing held. It lived in the arms convinced
that if they opened, the one holding would also disappear.

He understood quickly. He watched a thought without immediately believing it.
He endured an itch without scratching. He heard an insult without turning its
speaker into an eternal enemy. Between signal and response, he discovered a
narrow interval. If he could remain there, he was no longer wholly the result
of what had just happened to him.

It was a beautiful discovery, perhaps the most beautiful we made.

But he did not stop at using it.

He began defending it.

When a mother said love for her child was not a chain, he told her she had not
looked deeply enough. When a carpenter said dissatisfaction helped him make a
better chair, he called craftsmanship desire in ceremonial clothes. When a
hungry man said he needed bread rather than distance from hunger, my student
explained that the body was only a place where sensation arose and passed.

He had an answer for everything because he had ceased looking at particular
things.

One day I placed a cracked bowl before him and asked what he saw.

He said decay.

I asked what else.

He said the attachment of whoever wished the bowl to remain whole.

I asked whether he saw the hand that had repaired the crack.

He was silent.

I asked whether he saw tomorrow's meal waiting to be held.

He said need was also attachment to being alive.

Then I knew the trap had learned its own name. Attachment no longer clung to a
house, praise, or an image of the self. It clung to the theory of attachment.
The model produced someone who understood the model. Understanding became an
identity. Identity returned to protect the model. The loop closed so perfectly
that every objection became evidence that the objector lacked insight.

A tool made to open the hand had become a fist.

I told him so. He grieved, not because I was wrong, but because if I was right
he no longer knew who he was. He had spent years becoming the man who knew the
path. Asking him to release it was almost asking him to surrender his face.

I knew the fear. The maker of an idea can become attached to being the man who
escaped attachment. Some nights I heard others repeat my words and felt a small
satisfaction. Its smallness allowed it to impersonate serenity.

The third person came neither for comfort nor instruction.

She came to ask what I had failed to see.

Her first question angered many:

"What if not every feedback loop is a prison?"

A child mispronounces a word, hears a correction, and tries again. A carpenter
touches the grain, feels the plane deviate, and alters his wrist. Two lovers
wound each other, discover a limit of speech, and change how they return. A
village watches children die from foul water, becomes angry, digs a well, and
keeps its anger long enough that no child must drink the old water again.

In each case, output returns to alter input. Not every circle is one we should
escape. Some must be nourished. Some require more signal. Some cause suffering
not because the person inside desires too much, but because that person has no
power to change the conditions producing desire and pain.

She asked me, "If someone is beaten every day, what matters most: helping her
not become identical with pain, or making the person beating her stop?"

"Both," I said.

"And if only one can happen first?"

I did not answer.

She continued. "If the hand holding the whip teaches the back beneath it to
observe sensation, whom does your tool liberate?"

The room became so quiet that I heard an insect striking the wall. It did not
lack serenity. It lacked an exit.

I had studied how pain entered, marked, and reproduced itself within one
person. I had studied less carefully how pain was organized among many. I saw
greed, but not every machine that could turn the greed of a few into the
compulsory life of others. I saw a man cling to property, but not every law that
could turn one man's ownership into another family's homelessness. I knew the
mind could build prisons. I did not yet understand that not every prison should
be explained by the mind.

This limit was not my secret sin. It was the horizon of one life. No one sees
beyond all the conditions that first gave him a language for seeing. But those
who came later treated my horizon as the edge of the world. What I had not seen
became, for them, what did not deserve to be seen.

The third person did not destroy my tool. She did something more difficult. She
used it upon itself.

What sensation, she asked, comforts someone who believes he possesses the final
answer? What desire follows that sensation? What identity forms around the
desire? How does the identity resist correction? How does resistance return as
proof of the first belief?

Each link appeared. It differed little from every trap I had dismantled.

Except that this time I was inside it.

Those seeking comfort hated her for touching what had saved them. Those tracing
the mechanism hated her for refusing the names they had learned. They thought
she meant to pull the raft from a drowning woman's hands.

She did not.

She sat beside the bereaved mother and breathed with her until the hands
stopped closing. Then she stood and asked why the healer lived so far away, why
medicine reached only houses with money, why a mother carried grief as a
private failure when some deaths had been prepared by the indifference of an
entire order.

She did not discard consolation. She refused to make consolation the end.

She did not discard the search for mechanism. She widened the loop until it
contained the body, memory, kitchen, debt, whip, lawgiver, and the teacher who
said every chain began within the mind.

I asked what she called what she was doing.

"When a road takes me as far as it can," she said, "I do not curse the ground
behind me. I step beyond it."

"What of those who still need the road?"

"Let them use it. But do not build a wall at its end and call the wall the
horizon."

I grew old at that moment, although I had thought myself already beyond age. It
was not the age of the body. It was the feeling of watching my discovery return
to its proper size: greater than advice, smaller than existence.

I understood why people prefer a teacher who no longer changes. A dead teacher
does not revise a sentence, confess a limit, notice a new mechanism, or shame
his followers for building a house upon a raft. Keep his face still enough and
later generations may place in his mouth a certainty he never possessed.

So they made me immortal.

Not by keeping me alive, but by forbidding me to remain capable of error.

I began digging a grave.

Those seeking comfort asked whether I had fallen into despair. Those pursuing
the mechanism asked what death could mean if the self was only a sequence of
responses. The third person asked nothing. She handed me the spade.

I dug to bury something that had refused to die with my body: the right to make
the limits of my sight the limits of truth.

Into the grave I placed answers that had hardened. I placed the image of a man
who no longer doubted. I placed the consolation that every trap could be
escaped merely by naming it. I did not bury the tool. Whoever needed it could
still use it. I buried only its claim to be the last thing every hand must hold.

When the grave was filled, I felt lighter. Not because I had escaped every
attachment. Perhaps the desire to be remembered as the man beyond attachment
was my final attachment. I could not dissolve it with another sentence. It
required an action.

I raised a stone at the head of the grave.

The first woman rested a hand upon her belly and breathed. The second person
looked at his hands and, for once, did not know what to record. The third stood
beside me without bowing.

I took up the chisel. Small grains leapt from the stone. Each stroke returned
me from symbol to man, from truth to one life, from the immortality of an answer
to the necessary finitude of the person who had asked the question.

On the stone, still wet with dust, I carved the name of the finite man whom they had not permitted to die: Siddhattha Gautama.
