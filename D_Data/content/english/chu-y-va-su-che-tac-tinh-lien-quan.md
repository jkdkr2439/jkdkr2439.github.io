A person walks through a station while waiting for a message. The departure
board changes, a child drops a red glove, someone calls a name, rain begins
against the glass. The phone vibrates. For a moment the station disappears.

It has not ceased to exist. It has ceased to govern the field.

Attention is sometimes pictured as a lamp moved across an already completed
scene. The image is useful if we remember that a lamp does more than reveal.
It changes contrast. What enters the beam becomes easier to inspect, remember,
and connect with action. What remains outside may be present without becoming
available for judgment.

> Attention does not create importance from nothing, but it helps manufacture
> the experienced order of importance.

## Selection is necessary

A finite system cannot process every available difference with equal depth.
Selection is therefore not a moral failure. Without it, no task could hold
together. Reading requires suppressing the pressure of the room. Driving
requires placing some signals ahead of others. Conversation requires treating
one voice as relevant while many sounds continue.

The danger begins when necessity is mistaken for neutrality. Selection has a
history and a purpose. It follows bodily need, learned expectation, current
tasks, emotional pressure, social instruction, and the design of environments.

## Salience is not significance

Whatever captures attention tends to feel important. But capture and
importance are different relations.

A loud event may be trivial. A quiet trend may be decisive. A vivid anecdote
may occupy the mind more easily than a distribution. Repetition may produce
familiarity without producing truth. An interface may place one number in
large type and bury the denominator below a fold.

Salience answers, “What seized the system?” Significance answers, “What bears
on the question?” Good inquiry must repeatedly separate them.

## Attention has three economies

The first is a biological economy. Sudden movement, threat, pain, novelty, and
reward compete for limited processing.

The second is a practical economy. A task establishes what counts as signal.
The physician, electrician, editor, and thief may enter the same room and
notice different structures.

The third is a political economy. Institutions, platforms, schools, and
markets arrange visibility. They determine what is counted, displayed,
repeated, funded, and made costly to ignore. This does not mean attention is
centrally controlled. It means environments can profit from predictable modes
of capture.

## Blindness is not always absence

Failure to report a feature may mean that it was absent. It may also mean that
it was unattended, rapidly presented, masked by a task, conceptually
unavailable, or discarded before memory stabilized it.

Experimental work on inattentional blindness supplies a particularly clean
case: observers engaged in a demanding task can fail to report a fully visible
unexpected stimulus. A 2022 meta-analysis found only weak and uncertain
evidence that domain expertise reliably protects against this failure
(Ekelund et al., 2022, pp. 131 - 147).

This distinction matters whenever someone argues from silence. “Nobody
noticed” is not equivalent to “nothing occurred.” But the reverse warning is
equally important: possible inattention is not evidence that a preferred
event did occur.

Ignorance about attention should widen the hypothesis set, not authorize a
fantasy.

## Attention can be trained and audited

Training changes the field of relevance. A proofreader learns to see spacing.
A radiologist learns to discriminate patterns that initially appeared
featureless. Such gains are real, but specialization also creates habitual
blindness. The trained observer may force unfamiliar cases into familiar
patterns.

Before the audit, one further distinction matters. Attention can be captured,
directed, or cultivated. Capture occurs when a signal exploits an existing
sensitivity. Direction occurs when a task or authority tells the observer
where to look. Cultivation is slower: repeated practice changes what the
observer can discriminate at all.

A red notification captures. A manager directs. An apprenticeship cultivates.
Confusing the three produces ridiculous remedies. The captured person is told
to “focus harder,” as though private will had designed the signal. The
directed person is praised for noticing what the institution placed in the
center. The cultivated observer is accused of elitism because others cannot
yet see the distinction that years of correction made available.

## Relevance must answer to a question

Nothing is simply relevant. It is relevant to something: a task, a danger, an
explanation, a decision. When a speaker declares a detail irrelevant, ask for
the question under which it disappears.

This demand reveals when relevance is being used as a velvet rope around a
preferred conclusion. It also restrains the opposite vice, the collector's
belief that every neglected detail is a buried key. A detail earns return not
because it was ignored, but because it could alter the comparison among live
claims.

An attention audit therefore asks:

1. What was the task?
2. Which signals were made prominent?
3. What had to be suppressed for the task to succeed?
4. Who designed the field of visibility?
5. Which quiet variable could alter the conclusion?
6. What independent observer, measurement, or task would reveal another
   structure?

The aim is not permanent suspicion. It is controlled rotation. Change the
question, the observer, the scale, or the display, and inspect what returns.

Attention determines what receives enough processing to leave a trace. But a
trace that survives does not wait intact in a vault. The next chapter concerns
the present activity by which a past becomes available again.
