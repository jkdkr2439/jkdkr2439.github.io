## I. The Land That Did Not Know It Was Land

Before the surveyors came, the Tich Thuy floodplain possessed no shape respectable enough to receive a seal. In the dry months it was a long shelf of silt where children drove buffalo from the kapok tree to the reeds without presenting a pass. During the rains, half of it descended beneath the river. Perch entered the bean plots. Low graves rose from the water like old syllables the current had not finished reading.

The villagers named each place after something that had happened there: Widow Khe's Landing, where a woman once pulled three men out of a flood; the Egrets' Bed, white with birds in the eighth month; Broken-Pot Knoll, where soldiers of a forgotten reign had cooked before a forgotten battle. No one called the whole country property. One household owned a field, another a tree. The path was common. So was the bank. The dead retained a claim upon several low mounds which the living, for all their quarrels, had not yet dared to contest.

Rights lay across one another like roots: the right to gather fuel after harvest, to lead buffalo along the ridge, to lower a net when the water rose, to burn incense on a death day, to leave a passage through which an angry river might escape. A clerk would have seen confusion. An egret saw somewhere to land. A child saw a shortcut. The dead, had they remained interested in administration, might have judged that the living were managing tolerably well.

Later, in histories subsidised by the Khai Loi Mercantile Office, this period became the Age of Waste.

The name accomplished what three hundred soldiers might not. It gathered every use that produced no receipt and placed it inside emptiness. An old woman collecting greens became evidence of backwardness. A free landing became evidence of weak administration. A road held in the feet of seven generations was said not to exist because it had never applied for existence.

Power at Tich Thuy did not begin when the fence was raised. It began when one man received permission to define what was living as something not yet born.

## II. The Edict Concerning Emptiness

In the thirteenth year of the reigning king, the treasury had grown so thin that its keeper sat sideways to hide the bare shelf behind him. The court needed money. The ministers needed a word that did not smell of need. The Khai Loi Office needed land. The three requirements met within the gracious phrase national renewal.

The Minister of New Benefit described Tich Thuy as “an idle and uncultivated tract, not yet admitted into the nation’s circle of productive value.” The sentence entered the throne room perfectly clean. It carried neither mud nor the knock of an oar. An undersecretary supplied the reasoning: land which yielded no levy yielded no benefit; land which yielded no benefit was idle; idle land was value asleep, awaiting a capable hand.

No one asked whether the dead paid tax upon the ground containing their bones. No one asked whether the river, which deposited fertile silt for three villages each year, had been entered on the rolls of labour.

Luong Phuc, chief steward of the chartered office, knelt before the throne in garments of a humility woven from silk affordable only to the rich. He asked leave to turn the waste into a model new city. His office would make avenues, drains, a school, a theatre and employment. It would bring civilisation to a place which, until then, had merely contained inhabitants.

“What do you require?” asked the king.

“Only the right to measure.”

The court relaxed. He had requested no troops and, as yet, no land. A ruler sounds more innocent than a musket. They forgot that a musket can force a body to retreat, while a ruler joined to a seal can force the past itself off the map.

The edict conferred authority to survey, divide and “reveal latent value.” Each expression appeared to serve the realm. Yet survey contained the right to choose what deserved sight; divide contained the right to cut; value concealed a scale; latent allowed money to be collected from a future under no present obligation to exist.

Confucius might have asked whether the names had been rectified. Zhuangzi might have wondered how eager the fish were to be rescued from idleness. The court did not invite dead philosophers to its meetings. The dead are difficult to bribe and are usually misquoted by both parties.

The seal fell at noon. By late afternoon Tich Thuy looked unchanged. The grass did not know it had been renewed. The villages did not know that they had become the preliminary obstruction to a future city.

## III. The Stakes That Could Speak

The survey party arrived through morning mist. At its head walked the engineer Pham Chuan, who believed that most national misfortune began with a curved line. He brought a brass level, chains, twelve clerks and twenty labourers. A perspective painter followed them, for the mercantile office understood that land should first be measured by number and then measured again by desire.

Pham Chuan planted his instrument on Broken-Pot Knoll. Through the glass the world became obedient. Roof, bamboo, grave and water carrier were compelled between two crossing hairs. He did not hate the people. Hatred is crude, exhausting, and inclined to produce witnesses. He required only that they remain still long enough to become coordinates.

Stake One entered the soil beside the kapok tree. Stake Two stood in the buffalo path. Stake Seventeen pierced a turmeric bed. Stake Forty-three touched the rim of the grave of Widow Lua’s father-in-law. Each stake was painted red and crowned with a white number. The villagers gathered to hear these small objects speak in their place.

“What does it mean?” Widow Lua asked a clerk.

“It means the area has been determined.”

“Where was it before?”

The clerk regarded her with the compassion of a literate man for someone who had arranged a question incorrectly.

“It was here, but not precisely.”

Her husband had buried his father by the mark of a fig tree split by lightning. She knew the dead man’s head faced the mountain and his feet the river. This knowledge possessed no scale, engineer’s signature or value as security for a loan. It could not compete with a rectangle.

Soon the plain sprouted stakes. Children invented a race from Eight to Twenty-six until guards forbade them to trespass upon the newly delimited tract. For the first time in their lives, they trespassed upon the place of their birth.

The office retained a scholar, Nghe Bac, to compose an Inquiry into the Naturally Unowned Condition of Riparian Lands. He summoned Plato to prove the state should be directed by those able to perceive true forms; Aristotle to establish that the unfinished had not attained its end; and Mencius on constant property, omitting the lines that joined property to livelihood and mind. Geometry, he concluded, was the benevolence proper to an age of machines.

The Academy awarded the inquiry a medal. Three months later Nghe Bac received a plot overlooking a proposed garden.

There was no proof that the medal had purchased the argument. None was required to see the more elegant mechanism. The office produced a world in which praising it counted as understanding history, after which history rewarded such understanding with landed property. Corruption, when present, is a coin in the pocket. Definition is the pocket.

## IV. The Paper City

The Pavilion of Tomorrow opened in the capital before the first drain at Tich Thuy. Inside stood a model city half the size of a temple yard. White avenues passed beneath trees no larger than brush hairs. Villas wore red roofs. A theatre resembled a crown. A school opened its windows toward the sun. In the central square stood the pedestal for a statue whose subject had not been selected.

Visitors climbed a platform and inspected the future from the altitude of gods. No one had to lower himself to the level of the little houses. From above, a poor quarter and a cemetery were equally easy to replace with decorative moss.

The saleswomen wore the blue of hope. They called each buyer a founder of the city, although many intended to sell their deeds as soon as the price rose. Plots received names instead of coordinates: Poets’ Garden, Philosophers’ Walk, Centenary Lake, Eternal Hill. Men who had never read a poem competed for Poets’ Garden. Men unwilling to think of death paid deposits on Eternal Hill.

Luong Phuc stood before the model and announced that he did not sell land. He sold a manner of life.

The gazettes printed the phrase in large type. It was truer than its author intended. Mud was only the material. A manner of life was the more profitable commodity. Buyers paid to place distance between themselves and those who could not buy. The avenue allowed carriages to pass, but it also performed a finer service: it separated a household from the population whose poverty gave possession its social flavour.

Outside, a rickshaw puller named Ti waited for the guests. Through the glass he saw the model river, blue as a ribbon. The real river beside his lodging smelled of tanning liquor and dead dogs. The model contained no tannery, though the tannery owner had subscribed for three town houses. The art of the plan had solved pollution by excluding it from the scale.

A reporter asked when the school would be finished.

“In the third period.”

“And the infirmary?”

“The fourth.”

“The drains?”

“Essential works always receive priority.”

“In which period?”

“As soon as circumstances permit.”

The collection desk belonged to the first.

At night two hundred electric lamps illuminated the model. Three streets in the capital were darkened to provide the current. Their inhabitants sat without light and read the next morning that the kingdom had entered an age of illumination.

## V. Things That Would Not Fit a Rectangle

Three objects inconvenienced the approved plan: the burial ground, the road to the landing and the Dam family’s mulberry orchard.

The graves occupied the appointed site of the Triumphal Gate. The office offered free removal and a certificate of improved repose on a western hill, complete with ordered lanes, harmonious scenery and rising value as the district opened.

Widow Lua asked how the dead would enjoy the rise in value.

The minutes classified her intervention as emotional.

The landing road existed under hundreds of feet but not in the register. The office barred it, charged passage and proudly created employment for six gatekeepers. Previously no such employment had existed because no one had been prevented from passing. The roll of occupations increased as freedom of movement declined. The gazettes printed only the figure that knew how to dress for court.

The mulberry orchard was harder. The family possessed an old deed, but its boundary ran “from the stone resembling a tortoise to the place where the tower’s noon shadow touches the ditch.” The stone had entered a shrine foundation; the tower leaned; its shadow moved with the seasons. Pham Chuan judged the deed deficient in science.

“It was scientific enough to grow mulberries for sixty years,” said Grandfather Dam.

“Experience cannot replace a standard.”

“Can your standard replace a tree?”

The engineer marked the orchard disputed land, a phrase which transformed its inhabitants into one party in a quarrel with people who had not yet arrived.

That night Dam’s granddaughter Mien walked beneath the trees. Each had a shape in memory: one attacked by worms, one grafted by her father, one with a branch broken when her brother fell. The map was not false when it described all of them as mulberries. It was true at a resolution low enough to destroy an orchard.

A concept need not be false to become disastrous. It need only be enforced beyond the resolution that the discarded life can bear. Area was an accurate description. Once area received sole jurisdiction, roots, seasons, labour, memory and livelihood became boundary residue. Residue does not vanish. It returns as a widow without a grave, a child without a path, or a flood remembering its former course.

## VI. The Academy of Bitterness

While Luong Phuc sold tomorrow by the square measure, a highland merchant named Trac Da Tam sold a black and bitter drink. He began with three roasting pans and eventually erected a stone gateway so high that birds crossing it seemed to have entered a philosophy.

Da Tam believed the kingdom had weakened because its people had forgotten ambition. He printed sages upon his packages, founded libraries in drinking houses and required attendants to memorise remarks attributed to Napoleon, Laozi, Nguyen Du, Nietzsche and occasionally himself. Differences of century did not trouble him. Chronology, he believed, was a detail invented by men without a mission.

Visitors to the Academy of Bitterness climbed nine steps: Awakening, Resolve, Opening, Venture, Transcendence, Convergence, Service, Ascension, and Payment. The last stood beside the counter.

The capital’s scholars laughed at him. They laughed at the robes he designed, the carriages painted with a sun, and ceremonies of pouring that took longer than drinking. They were right. Da Tam was a theatre that hired its own audience, a prophet unwilling to admit that his doctrine carried a price list. He spoke of liberating the mind in the voice of a man who considered the key his private possession.

Yet his ridiculous library contained actual books. A blacksmith’s son read the Analects without paying. A village teacher found verses by Ho Xuan Huong removed from the respectable curriculum. A boy opened The Tale of Kieu and discovered that language could be beautiful enough to expose the price placed upon beauty. Da Tam wished to be remembered as the man who awakened a people; his vanity had the breadth of a square. To feed it, however, he had to construct several things upon which other minds could climb.

Luong Phuc called him wasteful.

The judgment was accurate. A library can fail. Books can be misunderstood. Pupils can oppose their master. Thought refuses to remain on the plot assigned to it. Land is more obedient. Once a purchaser borrows against the future, he becomes a guard posted over the story that makes his debt appear wise.

The two men shared an appetite for scale. Each wanted his name to outgrow his body. One counted roofs bearing his mark. The other counted minds containing a dream he had planted. Neither appetite was innocent. History may nevertheless distinguish between the danger that turns the world into inventory and the danger that turns it into a theatre of ideas.

## VII. A Banquet of Two Futures

They met at a banquet celebrating the second commencement of the Triumphal Gate. Its first foundation had sunk. The mercantile office described the event as a full-scale investigation of the soil.

Luong Phuc sat beside the minister. Da Tam sat beside a duchess who had subscribed for six plots but claimed to attend for art. Peacock, wine-steamed fish, foreign grapes and the Bitter Drink filled the table. Outside the kitchen, water carriers waited for the remains of the soup.

Asked to define civilisation, Luong Phuc praised visible order: straight roads, uniform roofs, secure deeds, moving capital, each man knowing his portion and never wandering into another’s.

Da Tam praised the inner flame: a people thinking greatly, each person drinking bitterness to wake from the small sleep of the fields.

“A flame requires a house,” Luong Phuc said.

“A house requires a soul,” Da Tam replied.

The minister smiled. The duchess applauded. Guests enjoy disagreements that leave their subscriptions undisturbed.

Mien served tea. Moving around the table, she heard Plato recruited for the plan, Confucius for rank, Adam Smith for appetite, Rousseau for nature inside a ticketed garden. Marx, not yet completely translated, appeared as a dangerous rumour from the docks. Nguyen Du was cited to prove that talent and wealth shared a destiny; the speaker had altered one syllable for the convenience of dinner.

Dead thinkers make economical mercenaries. They take no wages and cannot sue for distortion. Each has written enough for a later century to purchase the useful piece and declare that piece the ocean.

The minister asked Mien which future she preferred. The table laughed at his generosity.

“Which future still contains my family’s orchard?” she asked.

Luong Phuc mentioned compensation. Da Tam said loss was the price of a great undertaking. Different music, the same entrance fee.

“If greatness always requires another person’s house to vanish, why does it never begin with yours?”

No one laughed. The insolence of the poor is more serious than the appropriation of the rich. Society has laws for appropriation and only manners for speech.

Da Tam looked at her longer. Luong Phuc called the steward. Mien was removed, and the two men resumed their discussion of the national soul. The new tea girl walked more softly.

## VIII. The River Replies

The rains arrived late. The office announced that the unfinished waterworks had already proved effective, citing the increased number of dry days during the dry season.

Then the rain began.

Red water descended from the hills. It struck the wall of Poets’ Garden, sought the old landing road, and entered a new channel half the width of the former stream. Pham Chuan stood in the rain while his straight lines received a variable that had not read the plan.

On the second night the water crossed the embankment. On the third, the Triumphal Gate leaned like a hero who had discovered that his side was losing. Model villas went dark. Imported floorboards floated against the numbered stakes. Signs declaring ALREADY OWNED passed above the roofs of families whose right to inhabit the plain had never been recognised.

Water was not just. It entered poor houses first because they stood lower, drowned seed grain and broke earthen walls before reaching rich men’s cellars. Nature is no moral magistrate. It is a material order under no duty to alter its course for an elegant phrase.

Mien paddled through the orchard. A cradle hung against the office fence. It held no child, only a red blanket. Nearby, wire had opened the belly of Widow Lua’s buffalo, and blood loosened into a pink thread upon the water. Neither scene existed in the model. The model had not lied. It had been constructed so that the future could not flow backwards into the sales pavilion.

Da Tam opened his stores as shelters. He distributed blankets, boats and bitter drink. His photographer attended. The sun emblem appeared on every parcel of relief. A drowning person cares little which motive keeps a boat afloat. A survivor may accept the blanket and remember whose name was embroidered larger than the word relief. Both judgments can be true under different conditions.

From the capital Luong Phuc ordered that essential property be saved. The reply asked which property. He named the deed room, strongbox and city model. Records of ownership travelled upstairs before the sick below. This did not prove that Luong Phuc desired a death. It proved that his machine possessed a clearer chain of command for paper than for flesh.

Pham Chuan found Widow Lua clinging to a shrine roof and pulled her into his boat. For the first time he saw her without glass between them. Her face stood too near to be reduced by scale. Passing the submerged cemetery, she pointed to the graves of her husband and his father. The engineer said those positions disagreed with the removal plan.

“Has the water made a mistake too?”

He did not answer. Somewhere within him an instrument still tried to correct the world. The world had laid a wet hand upon the drawing.

On the fourth morning the embankment broke. Water entered the temporary pavilion and lifted the model. The theatre lost its roof. Eternal Hill spun away and lodged in a pigsty. The unnamed statue lost its head. Children collected the little villas and sailed them as boats.

It was the first time the future city had been put to use.

## IX. The Hearing on the Word Waste

The court established a Special Inquiry. Its chairman had signed the edict; its clerk had studied under Nghe Bac; its chamber was lent by the Khai Loi Office. This arrangement was praised as continuity of government.

Luong Phuc argued that the storm exceeded anticipation, works remained incomplete, unregistered dwellings obstructed drainage, and malicious rumours injured the confidence of buyers and sellers. Each claim contained some truth. The rain was exceptional. The works were unfinished. Houses stood in the path of water after construction had forced the water to find another path. Rumour lowered the price because price had always been supported by belief.

Pham Chuan presented heights, rates of flow and margins of error. The inquiry liked numbers; numbers did not weep while testifying. Asked why the old channel did not appear, he said it had been classified as intermittent.

“Does intermittent mean nonexistent?” Mien asked.

“It means insufficiently stable for the principal design.”

“A flood is intermittent.”

He lowered his eyes. He had once thought error was the small portion outside measurement. He now knew that an error might contain enough water to destroy the instrument.

Nghe Bac defended waste as a civilising category. Hesiod praised labour, he said; the Book of Changes praised transformation; Hegel described history as spirit coming to knowledge of itself. An old teacher asked whether Hegel had crossed Tich Thuy during a flood. The chairman forbade personal attacks upon an absent philosopher.

Mien placed a basket on the table: a ticket for the formerly free path, wire bearing buffalo hair, spoiled mulberries, a blurred burial certificate, a board from the model theatre, and a book rescued from Da Tam’s library.

“You called this land waste. Record the conditions. Waste to whom? In which season? Measured by what? For what purpose? Which forms of life had to disappear before the word became true?”

The question compelled every concept to disclose its birthplace, employer, instrument and list of exclusions.

Luong Phuc objected that government was impossible if every definition remained forever open.

He was right. A kingdom cannot halt every bridge while debating the essence of the opposite bank. Measurement must sometimes close, drawings must be signed, action must begin. But the distance between temporary closure and perpetual authority is the road every tyranny prefers to travel after dark.

Mien proposed five rules. Every operative definition should name its purpose and resolution. Those bearing the cost should enter the measure. What was excluded should remain recorded as boundary residue. Every judgment should carry a date of reopening. Whoever set a standard should bear part of the consequence when it failed.

The chairman called the rules dangerous.

“Dangerous to whom?” she asked.

This time the question had brought its conditions with it.

## X. Two Kinds of Monument

The inquiry concluded that no person bore complete responsibility. Everyone approved, for the sentence diluted guilt until no one had to carry it. The office paid less in fines than it had collected on its first day of subscriptions. Pham Chuan lost his post for six months and returned as adviser because no one else understood the machinery. Nghe Bac wrote a new inquiry on the ethics of error. The minister retired to a house held in his grandson’s name.

Luong Phuc renamed the undertaking the Rebirth of Tich Thuy. Disaster entered the advertisement as proof of resilience. Old subscribers paid for reinforcement; new ones were promised the lowest price. The Triumphal Gate became a Monument to Human Resolve, raised upon earth removed from a lower village.

Da Tam erected a statue of a woman holding a book against the flood. Its face resembled Mien, though no one had asked her. Beneath it ran the maxim: THE MIND RISES ABOVE EVERY FLOOD. What she had actually said was nearly the reverse: a mind deserves trust only when it remembers where water touched the body.

“I honoured you,” Da Tam told her.

“You honoured a woman easier to use.”

“A symbol must be concise.”

“So must a building plot.”

He grew angry because he possessed enough vision to feel the knife. Luong Phuc reduced land for sale; Da Tam reduced persons for myth. One divided ground, the other meaning. Their difference remained real, but not large enough to exempt the second man from examination.

At last he added a smaller inscription: THIS IMAGE IS THE MAKER’S VIEW; ITS SUBJECT RETAINS THE RIGHT TO OBJECT.

Visitors laughed. Students argued. Priests of ambition said the sentence profaned the monument. Da Tam regretted it on several nights but did not erase it. Perhaps the best part of aspiration lies not in the height it seeks, but in the opening through which another person may revise its meaning.

## XI. A Map with an Expiry

Tich Thuy became a city. History provided no clean ending. The dispossessed did not all return. The graves did not rise again. Seven mulberry trees survived. The avenue, once built, proved useful. The late infirmary saved lives. The third-period school taught children of families that had opposed the undertaking. A useful object can emerge from an unjust machine; its usefulness does not absolve the machine.

The city formed an Office of Residues. Every new map had to list what it did not display: seasonal paths, uncertain graves, livelihoods without deeds, rare watercourses, disputed memories and persons not consulted. A map expired after five years unless its definitions were reopened.

Officials detested the inconvenience. Financiers called it obstruction. Villagers sometimes invented memories to claim what had never been theirs. The office made errors, accepted gifts, and filled shelves with unread records. No institution escapes humanity by having been designed by humans.

Yet now and then an engineer opened the residue sheet and found beneath a perfect square a channel that appeared only once in three years. A judge discovered that ownerless meant inhabited by people denied the legal capacity to own. A child asked why an avenue bore Luong Phuc’s name while Widow Khe’s landing had no sign. Such questions did not overthrow order. They prevented order from mistaking the silence of what it had cut away for consent.

In old age Pham Chuan still carried a ruler. He had not lost faith in measurement. He had lost faith in the belief that numbers know their own limits. To every pupil he put one question:

“What portion of reality had to fall silent before this paper could speak so clearly?”

The question did not weaken the map. It gave the map a road home.

Mien died in mulberry season. Her stone carried the five rules and a sixth she later added: every power to revise a definition must disclose its own conditions. She understood how quickly the breaker of a cage may begin charging admission to the heap of iron.

Her legacy was no final definition of progress. It was a craft for preventing definition from becoming a throne.

## XII. Sellers of Ground and Horizon

Generations passed. The Khai Loi name appeared upon bridges, schools, infirmaries and streets. Some cursed it, some blessed it, and most lived by things it had built while paying for things it had cut away. Da Tam’s name remained on drinking houses, academies and sayings he may never have uttered. Some pupils became boasters, some teachers, and one mistranslated Nietzsche so productively that he founded a rather good school of poetry.

Posterity enjoyed asking which man had been greater. The question converted history into a contest of statues and seated the answerer as judge of time. Yet greatness by what measure: fortune, scale, the durability of symbols, the number drawn into a machine, the possibilities opened, or the generations required to repair the consequence?

If greatness means reproducing a saleable arrangement, Luong Phuc prevails. He understood that desire could be planned, distinction packaged, and tomorrow charged in advance. He fashioned a class of men who purchased a second house to defend the price of the first, then named this defensive circle faith in improvement.

If it means the reach of spiritual ambition, Da Tam stands higher. His absurdity was vertical: he wished to pull people beyond themselves. Luong Phuc’s practicality was horizontal: he wanted every measure of ground to breed another measure of price. One built too many gates into the horizon; the other too many fences around the earth. A gate may become a cult. A fence may become a city. Both require the question: who keeps the key?

That distinction is conditional. It holds if a mind enlarged is valued above scarcity optimised; if a library may produce readers who reject its founder; if material construction earns dignity by allowing those who bear its cost to enter the calculation. Change the conditions and the verdict may change. Conceal them and judgment becomes doctrine.

Human beings build the world twice. First with silt, timber, blood, water, muscle and time. Again with names. The second construction is lighter and is therefore mistaken for innocence. Yet the word waste can remove a village from conscience; renewal can make the dispossessed ashamed of obstructing tomorrow; vision can dress an appetite for scale in a philosopher’s robe; civilisation can persuade a fence that it is the dawn.

The deepest power does not always sit in a palace or strongbox. It occupies the editorial desk of reality, where someone decides which thing is a noun, which a footnote, which an admissible error. The holder of definition need not win each dispute. He designs the chamber, selects the measure, issues the entrance tickets and determines which answers count. By the time argument begins, his victory has foundations.

An attractive metaphor alone cannot oppose him. Shark, octopus, devil and Midas merely change the costume of anger. Satire acquires teeth when it shows the hand that turns a common path into a ticket; the measure that turns an orchard into area; the order that sends deeds upstairs before sick bodies; the phrase that collects money from an unbuilt school. Laughter should follow the mechanism. Then it becomes the sound made when a solemn machine loses its casing.

The seller of horizons deserves a blade as well. Thought, culture and ambition exceed pure profit, but their merchants may build empires from another person’s hunger for meaning. A poet is not clean because he writes poetry. A philosopher is not right because his questions are large. An enlightener is not innocent because he opens a library. Their worth lies partly in whether the instruments they leave permit later hands to oppose, revise, abandon and repurpose them.

The river still rises at Tich Thuy. It passes beneath a bridge named for a merchant, beside a library named for a visionary, and through a quarter inhabited by people absent from every monument. Water reads no philosophy, but performs an office philosophy performs when awake: it tests boundaries by pressure.

Children again discover a shortcut across grass not yet divided. They call it Kite Field. The land office calls it Development Reserve Seven. The Office of Residues records both names.

For the present, that is a civilisation.
