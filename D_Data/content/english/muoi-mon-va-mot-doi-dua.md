There are ten dishes on the table. You keep taking meat.

At the end you say, “That meal had no vegetables.”

It did. Your chopsticks did not go there.

We often speak as if the important things rise by themselves and stand in
front of us. They do not. We choose. Need chooses with us. Fear chooses. Habit
chooses. The question in our head tells the hand where to reach.

A mother hears her baby in a noisy room. A thief notices cameras. A buyer sees
the price. A tired driver misses a new sign on a road used for years. Each
person is in the same place, but not the same parts of the place become loud.

What you notice may be real. The meat was on the table. But failing to pick
something up does not make it absent.

This gets worse when the hand forgets it has a habit. If you always look for
insults, every talk becomes a plate of insults. If a report counts only what
fits its boxes, the uncounted part can vanish from the paper while remaining
alive in the street.

You cannot pick up everything. The answer is not to throw away the chopsticks.
It is to look at where they keep going.

Before trusting what feels most important, ask:

- What was I trying to find?
- What did that aim make easy to see?
- What did it make easy to miss?
- Who at the same table would reach elsewhere?
- What should I inspect before saying it was absent?

The bright part of the world is often the part your hand has learned to pick.
