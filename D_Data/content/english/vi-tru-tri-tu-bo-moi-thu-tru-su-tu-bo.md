*All characters and institutions in this work are fictional composites.*

I had gone to the Temple of the Inexhaustible Emptiness because I was assured
that nothing there was for sale. This was true in the religious sense. In the
commercial sense, everything was.

The evening had been arranged for the relief of the poor, though the poor had
not been invited, as their presence might have made the relief seem necessary.
The courtyard shone with lanterns. Beneath them stood tables of lacquered wood,
silver tea services, and ladies wearing the subdued colours by which wealth
announces that it has recently acquired taste. Each guest received a catalogue
entitled *Objects Without Attachment*. The catalogue was bound in silk.

I confess that I was delighted. A man who enters a temple to despise vanity
has merely chosen a spiritual form of vanity, and mine has always preferred
good lighting.

At the far end of the hall sat the Abbot of Perfect Release. He was a small,
serene man surrounded by a very large serenity. His chair was carved with
clouds, his name appeared in gold behind him, and a young attendant adjusted
the microphone so that his silence might be heard throughout the estate.

The master of ceremonies explained that the auction would allow us to practise
detachment. We were to detach ourselves from money; the temple, having greater
experience in emptiness, would undertake to receive it.

The first object was a plain clay cup from which the Abbot had once drunk
water. Its plainness had been certified. Bidding began modestly, for humility
is always cheap before society discovers who has used it. A merchant purchased
the cup for the price of a labourer's house and held it up with the expression
of a man who had defeated possession by obtaining the only example available.

The audience applauded. The Abbot lowered his eyes.

His humility was so complete that it required three cameras to record it.

Next came a length of wooden railing from an old meditation hall. The auctioneer
said it carried no miraculous power, which immediately doubled its value. A
woman in pearls asked whether keeping it in her home would improve the destiny
of her children. The auctioneer replied that destiny could never be bought.
He then suggested an opening bid.

The Abbot spoke at last. Desire, he told us, was the root of suffering.

Everyone became anxious to possess less desire than everyone else.

The bids rose beautifully.

They rose past generosity and entered reputation. They rose past reputation
and entered insurance. By the time the railing was sold, it no longer protected
any staircase, but several future lives had apparently acquired handrails.

The auction then presented a collection entitled *The Perils of Ordinary
Comfort*. It contained a hammock, a karaoke microphone, a young coffee tree,
and a travelling case. None had been blessed. Blessing would have made them
safe, and safety is fatal to the value of a warning.

The hammock was described as a machine upon which a diligent person might
accidentally erase an afternoon of merit. The microphone had belonged to a
singer who, having used his voice too freely in this life, had donated it to
protect the next one from silence. The coffee tree carried a subtler danger:
no one could explain precisely what moral debt it produced, which proved that
the debt had travelled beyond the reach of ordinary intelligence. As for the
travelling case, it reminded the young that curiosity about the world is often
punished by losing the ability to move through it.

The guests listened with the solemn relief of people discovering that pleasure
is dangerous chiefly when practised by someone else.

All four objects sold. The hammock fetched the highest price. Comfort has
always been most valuable to those publicly committed to distrusting it.

During the interval I met the Treasurer of Compassion. He had the grave and
exhausted manner of a man compelled to count what other people had renounced.
He explained the temple's system of merit to me. No payment guaranteed a
reward, he said; it merely established favourable conditions. I admired this
distinction. The law calls such language a disclaimer. Faith calls it depth.

There were silver, gold, and anonymous forms of giving. Anonymous gifts were
announced without the donor's name, though their amounts were displayed so
that anonymity would not become socially misleading. Larger offerings received
longer blessings. This was not because heaven preferred the rich. Heaven, like
every ancient institution, had simply accumulated ceremony around its major
accounts.

“And what of those who have nothing?” I asked.

The Treasurer smiled. “They may give sincerity.”

It seemed a harsh arrangement. The wealthy were allowed to give what they had
too much of; the poor were asked for the only thing they could not replace.

The second half began with a sermon on the afterlife. The Abbot described the
lower worlds with considerable administrative precision. There were chambers
for greed, chambers for false speech, chambers for disrespect, and one especially
uncomfortable department for people who questioned the necessity of
departments. Punishment varied by offence and duration. Appeals were not
mentioned.

I had never believed in hell, but by the fifth chamber I began to feel careless
for having arrived without coverage.

The Abbot did not threaten us. Threats are vulgar and awaken resistance. He
merely described eternity in the tone of a physician discussing an avoidable
condition, while the Treasurer placed donation envelopes upon every chair.
Fear, when shouted, is tyranny. Fear, when printed on fine paper, is guidance.

An elderly gentleman beside me selected the largest envelope. He whispered
that he wished to protect his grandchildren.

“From what?” I asked.

“From inheriting my karma.”

“And your property?”

He looked shocked. There are questions so impious that no religion has needed
to forbid them.

The penultimate lot was called the Seat of Emptiness, a cushion upon which the Abbot
had delivered his most celebrated discourse against the self. Its provenance
was impeccable. The self, we learned, did not exist; nevertheless, it had
signed the certificate.

Two industrialists contested the cushion. Each had spent his life expanding
an empire and had now discovered the spiritual importance of disappearing.
Neither was prepared to let the other disappear first. Their assistants bid
with disciplined calm while the audience watched liberation increase in exact
increments.

The winner was invited to the stage. The Abbot praised him for understanding
that ownership is an illusion. The man bowed, took possession of the cushion,
and asked that no one else be permitted to sit on it.

At this, I laughed. Unfortunately, I laughed alone.

The Abbot looked at me with kindness, which is the most elegant method by which
a powerful man can remind you that the room belongs to him. Then he laughed as
well. The room followed. For one miraculous instant no one knew whether we were
laughing at the buyer, the sermon, the Abbot, or ourselves. It was the only
truly communal act of the evening, and naturally it could not be itemised.

There was no final lot in the catalogue. The final lot walked onto the stage.

It was a family of four carrying the deed to their house. They lived beside the
temple, and the temple had recently discovered that its emptiness required more
parking. The father announced that they would surrender the house and move to
a small hut beyond the eastern wall. His children stood beside him with the
grave bewilderment of people too young to understand why an adult's faith had
already spent their inheritance.

The Abbot praised the gift as perfect trust. He explained that descendants of
such donors would prosper. This seemed considerate. The children had lost a
house in the present and were immediately granted wealth in a future no
surveyor could enter.

The guests rose to applaud. Even I rose. A seated conscience is easily noticed
in a standing room.

The father gave the deed to the Treasurer. The Treasurer gave him a framed
certificate. Thus a family exchanged the legal description of a house for the
moral description of having deserved one.

I found myself liking him. Hypocrisy is easy and therefore dull; he possessed
the rarer gift of believing every contradiction at the moment it benefited
someone. He believed wealth was empty when receiving it, sacred when spending
it, irrelevant when questioned, and indispensable when the roof required a
second ornamental roof. Consistency is a virtue for accountants. A spiritual
celebrity must offer a larger range of truths.

As the guests departed, each received a small card recording the merit of the
evening. Mine bore the lowest rank. I had purchased nothing, though I had
accepted expensive tea, enjoyed the merchant's embarrassment, and mistaken my
amusement for independence. The temple had measured me correctly. I too had
come to acquire superiority without paying the advertised price.

Outside the gate sat a beggar with a paper cup. He had been moved there so that
the arriving cars would not confuse charity with an obstacle. When the Abbot
emerged, the man raised his hand.

The Treasurer stepped forward, but the Abbot stopped him. He bent toward the
beggar and spoke softly. I moved close enough to hear, because curiosity is the
one appetite intellectuals rename before indulging it.

“Do not cling to merit,” the Abbot said.

The beggar considered this. Then he looked through the open gates at the empty
tables, the silk catalogues, and the donors carrying their newly purchased
renunciations to their cars.

He smiled.

Perhaps he had understood the doctrine. Perhaps he had understood the evening.
The distinction was too valuable for anyone inside the temple to afford.

I placed my merit card in his cup.

It was the most generous gift I made all night, and it cost him everything it
was worth.
