A doctor says a diagnosis is likely. A patient hears either “I have it” or “I
do not.” The interval between those sentences is where most reasoning lives.

Ordinary language encourages a binary picture of belief. We believe or
disbelieve, accept or reject. Some contexts require such commitments, but the
evidence beneath them is usually graded.

> Rational confidence should vary with the comparative support supplied by
> evidence. Commitment is sometimes binary; warrant rarely is.

## Confidence is not frequency

To assign high confidence to a claim is not necessarily to say that the claim
itself occurs frequently. A rare event can be strongly supported in one case.
A common event can be weakly supported in another.

Nor is confidence merely a feeling measured with numbers. It is an estimate
answerable to evidence, alternatives, and calibration.

## Base rates matter

Suppose a test detects most cases of a rare condition and has a small false
positive rate. A positive result may seem decisive. Yet if the condition is
very rare, false positives drawn from the large unaffected population may
remain substantial.

The point is not that base rates always dominate. New evidence can be highly
diagnostic. The point is that diagnostic force and prior prevalence perform
different jobs.

## Evidence changes odds

Bayesian language makes the structure visible:

```text
posterior odds = prior odds x likelihood ratio
```

The prior represents a claim's standing before the new datum. The likelihood
ratio represents how much more expected the datum is under one hypothesis than
another. The posterior is the revised standing.

One need not attach exact numbers to every belief. The structure remains
useful qualitatively: what did I believe before, how diagnostic is this
observation, and how far should it move me?

## Calibration tests confidence

A person is calibrated when claims assigned roughly the same confidence are
correct at roughly that rate across suitable cases. Calibration does not
guarantee good discrimination. Someone who always predicts the common outcome
may be calibrated in a crude sense while failing to identify important
differences.

Calibration and discrimination are distinct dimensions of accuracy: a judge
may separate higher-risk from lower-risk cases while systematically assigning
probabilities that are too high or too low. Reviews of clinical prediction
therefore warn against reporting discrimination while leaving calibration
unexamined (Lindhiem et al., 2020, pp. 840 - 854).

Good judgment needs both calibration and resolution: confidence should match
success, and it should change when relevant evidence changes.

## False precision is not rigor

Writing “73.4 percent” does not make an estimate scientific. Exactness must be
earned by a model, data, and stable measurement. Otherwise a range or verbal
category may communicate more honestly.

But vague language can also hide. “Maybe,” “possibly,” and “cannot be ruled
out” cover enormous intervals. Consequential communication should say enough
to guide interpretation: unlikely, plausible, more likely than not, highly
likely, and what evidence supports the category.

## Confidence has a social costume

People rarely announce only a probability. Tone, title, fluency, and setting
lend confidence a costume. A hesitant expert may sound weaker than a practiced
salesman. A number printed to two decimal places may appear more disciplined
than a careful range. The audience then evaluates the costume because the
underlying calibration is expensive to inspect.

This makes rhetorical confidence an epistemic variable. It should neither be
ignored nor mistaken for accuracy. Hesitation may reveal access to complexity;
it may also reveal ignorance. Fluency may arise from mastery; it may also arise
from repeating the same mistake for ten years.

Ask not only how certain the speaker sounds, but what happened on previous
occasions when the speaker sounded this certain.

## Updating should have a memory

A person can claim to revise with every new fact while repeatedly returning to
the same favored conclusion. Genuine updating has a ledger. It records the
prior position, the diagnostic force assigned to the evidence, and the
posterior change.

Without such memory, revision becomes theatre. Yesterday's certainty is
quietly replaced by today's certainty, and the speaker congratulates himself
for having always followed the evidence. A mind that updates but never records
its former odds cannot be calibrated; it can only be impressed.

## A confidence audit

1. What are the live alternatives?
2. What was their standing before the new evidence?
3. How expected is the evidence under each alternative?
4. Are repeated observations independent?
5. Is the confidence numerically earned or cosmetically precise?
6. How well calibrated is the source in similar cases?
7. What new evidence would move confidence substantially?

Belief guides action, but confidence alone does not determine what to do.
Acting at 60 percent may be prudent in one situation and reckless in another.
The missing variable is the cost of error.
