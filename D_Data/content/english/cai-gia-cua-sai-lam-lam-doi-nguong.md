A smoke alarm rings at night. The chance of a fire may be small. The cost of
checking is smaller.

A court considers punishment. The cost of a false conviction is severe. The
same probability that justified leaving the house cannot justify taking away a
person's liberty.

Truth has not changed between these cases. The threshold for action has.

> Practical stakes can change how much evidence is required before acting,
> without changing whether the underlying proposition is true.

## Two errors, two costs

Every binary decision creates at least two possible mistakes:

- act as if the claim is true when it is false;
- act as if the claim is false when it is true.

These are often called false positives and false negatives. Their costs are
rarely symmetrical. Screening may tolerate more false positives when missing a
treatable disease is dangerous. Punishment may demand a higher threshold when
the sanction is grave.

Reasoning becomes clearer when the errors are named separately.

## Belief and action must not be collapsed

A person may reasonably believe rain is more likely than not and still leave
the umbrella at home because carrying it is costly. Another may carry it at a
small chance of rain because getting wet would ruin equipment.

The action does not reveal a hidden factual belief unless stakes are known.
Likewise, choosing caution does not prove that danger is probable. It may show
only that one outcome is intolerable.

## Values enter through consequences

Decisions require judgments about what outcomes matter, to whom, and over what
time. Such judgments cannot be disguised as measurements. A model may estimate
the probability of flooding. It does not by itself decide how to distribute
protection, displacement, or cost.

Bringing values into the open does not corrupt inquiry. Hidden values are more
dangerous because they can masquerade as facts.

## Precaution has limits

“Better safe than sorry” is useful when precaution is cheap and potential harm
is large. It fails as a universal rule because precautions also impose costs,
risks, and lost opportunities. Several incompatible precautions may compete.

The correct question is not whether caution is good. It is which error each
policy guards against, which new errors it creates, and how burdens are
distributed.

## Reversible actions need less certainty

When a decision is cheap, reversible, and informative, acting under uncertainty
can be rational. A provisional step may itself generate evidence. Irreversible
or coercive decisions require stronger support because correction cannot fully
restore what was lost.

This produces a practical principle:

> Lower the threshold for reversible inquiry; raise it for irreversible harm.

## Thresholds can distribute dignity

A threshold is not merely a number. It determines who must bear uncertainty.
When an institution acts on weak evidence, the person acted upon may pay for
its caution. When it refuses to act without near certainty, another person may
pay for its restraint.

This is why “we need more evidence” can be either wisdom or a policy of
delay. The phrase is incomplete until it identifies who remains exposed while
evidence accumulates, what evidence is realistically obtainable, and whether
the demand is applied symmetrically.

The same applies to precaution. A decision-maker may protect himself from
blame by transferring risk to people who do not sign the form. Bureaucratic
prudence is often personal safety wearing the institution's coat.

## Decisions can purchase information

Some actions do more than respond to uncertainty; they change it. A pilot
program, reversible trial, temporary separation, or additional measurement can
produce evidence while limiting harm.

This gives inquiry an active form. Instead of waiting for certainty to arrive,
design an action whose possible outcomes discriminate among claims. The action
should be small enough to reverse and clear enough to learn from.

An experiment that cannot alter the decision is decoration; a provisional
measure that automatically becomes permanent was never provisional.

## A decision-threshold audit

1. What decision must be made?
2. What factual claim bears on it?
3. What are the false-positive and false-negative outcomes?
4. Who bears each cost?
5. Is the action reversible?
6. Can a smaller action gather more information?
7. Have value judgments been stated separately from probability estimates?

Action closes a question provisionally; inquiry must remain capable of
reopening it.
