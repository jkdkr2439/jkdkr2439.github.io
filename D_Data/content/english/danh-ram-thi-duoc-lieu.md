You feel pressure in your belly. You think it is only gas.

If you are wrong in an empty room, the price is a pair of trousers.

If a surgeon is wrong before cutting you open, the price is different.

The amount of support needed for a choice depends partly on what a wrong
choice can destroy. We accept a rough guess when choosing lunch. We should not
use the same roughness to convict a person, remove an organ, or move a city.

This does not change what is true. Risk does not make a disease appear. It
changes how careful we must be before acting as if one answer is settled.

There are two prices: acting when the claim is false, and failing to act when
it is true. A fire alarm can be wrong in both directions. Too sensitive, and
it empties the building for toast. Too dull, and it stays quiet for fire.

A restaurant faces the same pair. Throwing out safe food wastes money. Serving
spoiled food can hurt people. The owner cannot make both errors impossible.
The choice of a test and a cutoff decides which error becomes easier.

This is why one amount of support can lead to different acts. A doctor may
order a cheap, safe test while still unsure. The same doctor may refuse a
dangerous operation until much more is known. The belief did not secretly
change. The act and its price changed.

Beware of people who demand perfect certainty only when action would hurt
their interests. Waiting also acts. It spends time and may leave harm running.

Before deciding, ask who pays if you are wrong, how large the loss is, whether
it can be repaired, and what waiting will cost.

Use evidence to judge the claim. Use the price of error to set the bar for the
act.
