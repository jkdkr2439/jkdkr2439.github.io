Two people stand on opposite sides of a closed door. One sees smoke entering
the corridor. The other hears glass break inside the room. Neither is
hallucinating. Neither possesses the event.

The first person may be right that something is burning. The second may be
right that a window has broken. Their reports can be accurate without being
complete, and incomplete without being useless. The elementary problem of
knowledge begins in this space between contact and possession.

A finite knower does not receive a whole state of affairs at once. The knower
encounters it from somewhere, at some time, through capacities and instruments
with a determinate range. What arrives is constrained by the world, but it is
also conditioned by the route through which it arrives.

This chapter defends a deliberately limited thesis:

> Access to reality is real, but finite. What a subject receives must not be
> silently identified with everything there is to receive.

The thesis does not imply that reality is unknowable, that perception is a
private film projected inside the head, or that every interpretation is as
good as every other. It asks for a simpler discipline: before assessing a
claim, reconstruct the path by which the relevant part of the world became
available to the person making it.

## 1. Five things that should not be collapsed

Discussions of knowledge often move too quickly because they treat several
different items as though they were one.

The first is the **state of affairs** under investigation: the room, the fire,
the broken glass, the sequence of events, and the relations among them.

The second is a **channel of access**: vision, hearing, touch, an instrument, a
record, a witness, a photograph, or a chain combining several of these.

The third is a **trace** produced through that channel: a pattern of light, a
sound, a numerical reading, a stored image, a mark in a record, or a
sentence uttered by a witness.

The fourth is a **representation or model** organized from available traces.
It may be a perceptual organization, a memory, a graph, a causal story, or a
verbal description.

The fifth is a **claim**: there is a fire; someone broke the window; the alarm
failed; a particular person caused the damage.

These five items are related. They are not interchangeable.

A reading of 39 degrees is not a fever. It is a measurement that may count as
evidence of fever under appropriate conditions. A photograph is not the event
it records. A witness's sentence is not the past. A theory is not the process
it explains. A correct sentence is not made of the same material as the fact
that makes it correct.

The point is not merely linguistic. Each transition introduces a new question.
Was the channel within range? Was the instrument calibrated? What did the
frame exclude? How was the trace organized? Which alternatives were ignored?
How far does the final claim travel beyond what was directly available?

## 2. A minimal model of access

The sequence can be represented abstractly:

```text
R → C(R) = S → M(S) → Q
```

Here `R` is the relevant state of reality, `C` is a channel, `S` is the trace
produced through that channel, `M` is a model constructed from the trace, and
`Q` is a claim made from the model.

The notation is not a theory of mind. It does not decide whether perception is
direct, representational, inferential, embodied, ecological, or predictive.
It marks distinctions that several competing theories can recognize.

A direct realist may insist that ordinary perception is a direct relation to
worldly objects rather than awareness of an internal intermediary. That
position need not deny that a perceiver has a location, a visual field, a
range of sensitivity, and conditions under which perception succeeds or
fails. A representationalist may give a richer role to perceptual content. A
Kantian may emphasize conditions contributed by the form of human cognition.
The minimal claim of this chapter does not settle that dispute. It says only
that finite access has conditions and a scope.

This restraint matters. The history of epistemology contains powerful
arguments that begin from the difference between appearance and reality and
then move toward stronger conclusions. Russell's *The Problems of Philosophy*
opens with variations in how a table appears from different positions and
lighting conditions, then asks what, if anything, may be known of the table
itself. Kant argues, in a very different framework, that possible experience
is conditioned by forms and concepts of cognition. These are not two versions
of the same doctrine. They are evidence that the route from appearance to
reality has generated distinct and incompatible philosophical systems.

The present argument should therefore remain smaller than either system.

## 3. Four limits of a channel

### Range

A channel is responsive to some differences and not others. Human eyes do not
detect every wavelength. A microphone has a frequency range. A survey records
only what its questions permit respondents to express. A database contains
the fields someone decided to collect.

What lies outside the range may still be causally active. Its absence from the
record is not evidence of its absence from the world.

### Position

Access occurs from somewhere. Spatial position determines what is occluded.
Temporal position determines which stages of a process are available. Social
position may determine which practices, risks, or meanings are familiar
enough to be noticed.

Situated access does not mean that every position manufactures its own
reality. It means that positions disclose and conceal different relations.
Multiple positions may correct one another precisely because they concern a
shared world.

### Resolution

A channel distinguishes differences only down to some scale. An average may
describe a population while hiding its distribution. A diagnostic category
may group cases that differ in mechanism. A word such as *crowd* may be useful
while suppressing the differences among the people it names.

Compression is not necessarily an error. Thought and measurement require it.
The error is forgetting which distinctions were compressed.

### Transformation

A channel does not always preserve the form of what it registers. A
thermometer converts thermal conditions into a reading. A microphone converts
pressure variation into an electrical signal. A map converts terrain into
symbols and scale. Memory makes a past event available through a present act
of remembering.

Transformation can increase knowledge. A numerical reading may be more
precise and shareable than a bodily impression. A map may reveal a relation
that no single view from the ground displays. The important question is not
whether transformation occurred, but whether its rule, limits, and possible
failures are understood.

## 4. The inverse problem

Knowledge often requires moving backward from a trace to the state that
produced it.

Smoke may indicate fire, but different processes produce smoke. Silence may
indicate anger, fatigue, fear, restraint, distraction, or failure to hear. A
rising number may reflect a change in the target phenomenon, a change in
measurement, a different sample, or a third factor affecting both.

This creates an inverse problem: more than one possible state may be
compatible with the trace presently available.

Hume's treatment of causal reasoning places severe pressure on any effortless
move from what has been observed to a necessary connection or an unobserved
future. One need not accept every part of Hume's solution to preserve the
lesson. Observation does not announce its own unique explanation.

The proper response is not paralysis. It is discrimination.

When several explanations fit the present trace, inquiry asks what further
observation would make them diverge. It seeks a test whose possible results
would support one alternative more strongly than another. It may retain
several hypotheses, assign them different levels of confidence, and state
which assumptions make the ranking possible.

Ignorance becomes productive when it is localized.

## 5. Instruments extend access without abolishing it

Scientific instruments are sometimes described as devices that allow us to
overcome the limits of the senses. That description is partly right and
potentially misleading.

A microscope makes structures available that unaided vision cannot resolve.
A telescope brings traces from distant objects into a usable range. A
recorder preserves patterns after the original event has passed. Statistical
methods reveal relations not visible in isolated cases.

Yet an instrument does not replace conditioned access with a view from
nowhere. It creates a longer and often better controlled chain:

```text
world
→ sensor
→ transduction
→ calibration
→ processing
→ display
→ interpretation
```

The chain may be more reliable than unaided judgment because its
transformations can be standardized, tested, repeated, and compared. Its
strength lies not in having no conditions, but in making conditions explicit
and correctable.

An instrument is epistemically valuable when we know what it responds to, how
its output changes with its input, where it saturates, how it is calibrated,
which noise it introduces, and what independent checks are available.

Tools do not make knowers infinite. They make some limits measurable.

## 6. Three objections

### If access is conditioned, do we know only our own representations?

That conclusion does not follow from the minimal thesis. A trace can differ
from its source while remaining causally and systematically constrained by
it. A footprint is not a foot, but it can provide knowledge of a foot. A
measurement is not the property measured, but a calibrated relation may allow
the property to be estimated.

Mediation does not entail isolation.

### If access is partial, are all interpretations equally valid?

No. Interpretations differ in fit, predictive success, explanatory reach,
stability across channels, sensitivity to counterevidence, and capacity to
support successful intervention.

The fact that no map contains the entire territory does not make every map
equally useful. One may omit restaurants because it is a geological map.
Another may place the river on the wrong side of the city. Omission relative
to purpose and error relative to the world are not the same thing.

### Is this too elaborate for ordinary knowledge?

Ordinary people already inspect access conditions when consequences matter.
They ask: Did you see it yourself? Who told you? When was the photograph
taken? Was the scale working? What happened before the recording began? Could
there be another explanation?

These are epistemological questions in working clothes.

The aim of analysis is not to replace ordinary judgment with ceremony. It is
to make ordinary caution portable across situations in which intuition alone
is unreliable.

## 7. Assertion should be proportional to access

The most important practical result is a rule of proportionality:

> The scope and confidence of a claim should not exceed the reach and quality
> of the access that supports it.

A single encounter may justify a report about that encounter. It rarely
justifies a claim about a person's permanent character. A small sample may
support a description of the sampled cases. It does not automatically support
a claim about a population. A photograph may establish that something was
visible inside its frame at a particular moment. It does not, by itself,
explain the event.

This rule does not demand weak language for its own sake. Adding *perhaps* to
every sentence is not intellectual humility. It is verbal fog.

Good uncertainty is specific. It identifies:

- what was observed;
- through which channel;
- under which conditions;
- what remains unobserved;
- which inferential step is contestable;
- what evidence would change the assessment.

The point is not to sound unsure. It is to know where uncertainty lives.

## 8. The prestige of possession

The language of knowledge easily becomes the language of property. We *have*
the facts. We *possess* the truth. An opponent has nothing. The metaphor is
convenient and occasionally poisonous.

Possession suggests that knowledge can be detached from the route by which it
was obtained and carried intact into every new situation. But a warranted
claim has conditions of custody. Its strength depends on the access, method,
alternatives, and scope that licensed it. Remove those conditions and the same
sentence may continue to sound true after its warrant has expired.

This is how intellectual status survives intellectual contact. A person
learns a conclusion in one domain, transports it beyond the instrument that
earned it, and treats resistance as trespass. The fact has become property,
and criticism now resembles theft.

Knowledge is better imagined as a maintained relation. It can be stable,
shareable, and strong. It can also decay when its source disappears, its
measurement changes, or its domain is exceeded. The responsible knower does
not clutch a conclusion more tightly. He preserves the route by which the
conclusion may be checked.

## 9. A seven-question access audit

Before accepting or making a consequential claim, ask:

1. What part of the situation was actually available?
2. Through which channel did it become available?
3. What falls outside that channel's range?
4. How did position, resolution, or transformation shape the trace?
5. Which alternative states could produce a similar trace?
6. What further observation would distinguish those alternatives?
7. Does the claim travel farther than its support?

The audit does not produce certainty. It produces a map of the claim's
dependence on the world, the channel, and the steps between them.

## 10. From access to perception

This chapter has treated the channel abstractly. But perception is not merely
a pipe through which information passes. A perceiver is a living body with
capacities, needs, expectations, and possibilities for action. The world
constrains perception, while the organization of the perceiver helps
determine what becomes salient and what can appear at all.

Finite access therefore creates the next question:

> When a subject perceives, how much belongs to the world encountered, and how
> much belongs to the form of the encounter?

That is the problem of Chapter 2.
