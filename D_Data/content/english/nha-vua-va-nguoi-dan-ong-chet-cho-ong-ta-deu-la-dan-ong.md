<p>Modern arguments about gender often begin with a useful truth and then flatten it into a convenient picture. Men have disproportionately occupied formal positions of political, military, religious, and economic authority. From that fact, it is tempting to draw a single line around all men and call the enclosed population the beneficiary class. Yet a throne and a trench are both historically male spaces, and they do not distribute the same reward.</p>

<p>The man wearing the crown and the man ordered to die for it are both men. Only one is usually offered as evidence that men possess power.</p>

<p>This does not erase the exclusion, coercion, or violence women have endured. It changes the resolution of the map. A hierarchy built largely by men can subordinate women while also consuming enormous numbers of men. High-status male coalitions may control property, institutions, and female autonomy; lower-status males may be recruited as labor, muscle, soldiers, rivals, prisoners, and expendable material. “Men rule” can therefore be true at one scale and badly misleading at another.</p>

<p>The deeper mechanism begins before ideology. In sexually reproducing species, male and female reproductive roles are not initially symmetrical. Eggs are generally more energetically expensive than sperm; in mammals, pregnancy, birth, and often lactation add further costs borne by females. These asymmetries do not dictate a moral purpose, a social destiny, or a universal psychology. They alter the recurrent costs attached to risk, injury, mate competition, and parental survival.</p>

<p>In many species, a male can potentially increase reproductive success by gaining access to additional mates. This can intensify male-male competition, especially where reproductive success is unequally distributed. Contest does not produce solidarity among males. It produces rank, alliance, exclusion, display, injury, and sometimes death. The rival most likely to seize a male's territory, status, mate, or accumulated resources is often another male.</p>

<p>Human beings inherit these pressures but do not merely obey them. We convert them into myths, uniforms, inheritance rules, marriage systems, armies, corporations, and states. Biology provides gradients; culture builds roads down them. The result is not a universal conspiracy of men against women. It is more often a shifting coalition structure in which some men coordinate power, women negotiate or resist constraints, and many other men compete for admission or are spent defending the arrangement.</p>

<p>The empirical pattern is blunt. UNODC reported that men accounted for 81 percent of intentional homicide victims globally in 2021 and roughly 90 percent of suspects brought into formal contact with police for intentional homicide. Men are heavily represented on both sides of lethal violence. This is not evidence that male suffering matters more, nor that male perpetrators should be excused. It reveals that male violence is frequently directed at males, and that the category “men” contains both the hand that holds the weapon and much of the flesh it enters.</p>

<p>Research also finds that average sex differences depend strongly on the kind of aggression, the target, and the stakes. Males show more direct and escalated aggression on average, particularly outside kinship and as risk rises. Testosterone is associated with aggression only weakly and contextually; it is not a chemical commandment. Women compete and aggress as well. In some contexts, such as sibling aggression, familiar sex differences can disappear or reverse. During lactation, maternal defense can intensify sharply in many mammals, and experimental work has found heightened defensive aggression among breastfeeding women under particular laboratory conditions.</p>

<p>The difference, then, is not “men can kill and women cannot.” A more useful distinction concerns protective attention.</p>

<p>One protective strategy locks onto the source of danger: identify the threat, approach it, disable it, and if necessary destroy it. Another locks onto the life at risk: move it, conceal it, feed it, preserve the caregiver, recruit allies, and survive long enough to protect again tomorrow. Both sexes can use both strategies. Yet reproductive costs, physical dimorphism, social training, and historical division of labor may alter their average availability and prestige.</p>

<p>Civilizations love the first strategy because it photographs well. A warrior standing over a defeated enemy is easy to carve in stone. The person who moved children before the raid, stored food, maintained alliances, treated wounds, and prevented the next conflict leaves fewer dramatic silhouettes. History gives medals to visible danger and footnotes to invisible continuity.</p>

<p>This produces a seductive male fantasy: if I destroy the threat, my children will be safe. If I die doing it, their mother remains to preserve the future. At a narrow time scale, the reasoning may be correct. Some dangers cannot be negotiated with. A defender unwilling to use force can hand the lives in his care to an aggressor's mercy.</p>

<p>But the model becomes foolish when it mistakes the attended threat for the entire field of threats.</p>

<p>The enemy before me may belong to a coalition. His death may trigger retaliation. Removing him may create a power vacuum. My own death may deprive the community of income, knowledge, authority, affection, and future defensive capacity. The children I save today may inherit trauma, debt, vendetta, or a weakened system tomorrow. Victory over one danger can reduce resistance to five dangers that have not yet acquired names.</p>

<p>This is the logic of all-in behavior. It is not always courage. Sometimes it is low-resolution cognition wearing heroic armor. The world is simplified into one enemy, one battle, one decisive sacrifice. Uncertainty disappears because the defender has converted a living system into a duel.</p>

<p>A mediocre man dreams of dying in the final battle. A capable guardian understands that his community must never be reduced to only one final battle.</p>

<p>The distinction resembles portfolio risk and resilient engineering. A system that commits every resource to correcting one disturbance may successfully remove it and still collapse when the next disturbance arrives. Robust systems retain redundancy, memory, repair capacity, reserves, and alternative paths. They do not worship efficiency so completely that a single success leaves them unable to survive.</p>

<p>Mature protection therefore asks more than whether a threat can be destroyed. It asks what will occupy the space afterward; which capabilities will remain; whether retaliation has been modeled; whether the intervention is reversible; and whether the community will still be able to learn, feed, repair, reproduce, and defend itself after the hero has finished being heroic.</p>

<p>This returns us to power. What is commonly called patriarchy may be examined not as a benefit distributed equally to males, but as a system that allocates different functions and risks. Elite males may receive authority, reproductive opportunity, property, and symbolic immortality. Many other males receive conditional status in exchange for production, obedience, and willingness to face danger. Women may be protected, controlled, exchanged, excluded, or politically mobilized according to the same system's needs. Every group contains agency and variation, but the architecture is not a simple contest between two unified sexes.</p>

<p>The most durable hierarchies convince their expendable members that expendability is honor. A young man is told that dying proves his worth; a young woman is told that being controlled proves her value. Both injuries can be decorated as protection. The system survives by turning imposed functions into identities people defend as if they had authored them.</p>

<p>None of this makes biology irrelevant. Nor does biology make reform futile. Human institutions are precisely the mechanisms by which inherited pressures are redirected. Courts can replace vengeance. Elections can replace succession by bloodshed. Professional defense can be placed under law. Property can be separated from ownership of persons. Care can be recognized as productive labor. Men can be fathers rather than merely genetic contributors; women can command rather than merely be protected. Civilization is not the disappearance of instinct but the construction of feedback around it.</p>

<p>The goal is not to feminize men into harmlessness or masculinize women into violence. A community without the capacity to stop predation survives only by permission of predators. A community that knows only how to destroy eventually classifies disagreement, weakness, and difference as predation. It needs both the power to terminate a genuine threat and the intelligence to preserve life beyond the encounter.</p>

<p>The highest form of protective strength is therefore not the readiness to die. Death may require a moment of courage, rage, despair, or obedience. The harder task is to remain alive and responsible across time: to distinguish danger from insult, necessity from vanity, defense from possession, and the survival of the community from the survival of one's own authority.</p>

<p>The weak man goes all in because he sees one enemy. The strong guardian preserves enough force for the dangers that have not yet introduced themselves.</p>

<p>And the wise society does not build its future around the fantasy that one sex creates while the other destroys. Creation can become possessive. Protection can become domination. Aggression can defend life, and care can quietly enable tyranny. Functions must be judged by what they do to the living system, not by the gender of the body performing them.</p>

<p>The king and the man who dies for him are both men. The mother who hides a child and the mother who attacks an intruder are both women. These facts do not cancel one another. They reveal a larger field in which sex, rank, kinship, resources, institutions, and time continually rearrange what protection means.</p>

<p>The question is no longer simply who held power, or which sex suffered. It is: who controlled the system, who was converted into its resources, what form of protection it rewarded, and whether the victory it purchased left anyone capable of protecting the future.</p>
